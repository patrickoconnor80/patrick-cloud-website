Eyes & Vision

Health GoalMyopiaMacular degenerationDry eye

Three different problems take most people's sight. In children, the eyeball grows too long (short-sightedness, or myopia). In older adults, the retina wears out (macular degeneration) or its blood vessels fail (diabetes). In between, the eye's surface dries out. Each has its own fix, and the best-proven ones are free: daylight for children, normal blood sugar, and not smoking.

A clear-eyed week

  • Kids outdoors about 2 hours a day, and adults get morning daylight too
  • 2–3 eggs a day, plus a big salad or cooked greens
  • Wild salmon or sardines 2–3 times
  • Every 20 minutes at a screen, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds
  • A hat or sunglasses for long days on water, snow or sand
  • An eye exam every 1–2 years after 40, yearly if you have diabetes

Watch out

  • Sudden flashes, a shower of floaters or a curtain over your vision is an emergency (possible retinal detachment)
  • Straight lines looking wavy can be wet macular degeneration. See a doctor within days.
  • A painful red eye with blurred vision needs same-day care
  • Former and current smokers shouldn't take beta-carotene

How it fits together

The eye focuses light through the cornea and lens onto the retina. Its centre, the macula, gives you sharp reading and face vision. Short-sightedness happens when the eyeball grows too long during childhood, so distant images focus in front of the retina. Bright daylight releases dopamine in the retina, which slows that growth. The macula has the highest oxygen use and the most light exposure of any tissue. It protects itself with a yellow pigment (lutein and zeaxanthin) and constant recycling, and wears out with smoking, oxidative stress and age. The retina's tiny blood vessels are the first to suffer from high blood sugar. On the outside, a thin film of tears, oil and mucus keeps the cornea clear. It breaks down when you don't blink or the air is dry.

Myopia is set in childhood, macular disease builds over decades, and dry eye is day to day. Act on the one that fits your age.

Get children outside

Short-sightedness has exploded in East Asian cities, and it's rising everywhere children spend their days indoors. The cause is mostly too little daylight, not reading itself. Outdoor light is 10–100 times brighter than indoor light, and it drives the release of retinal dopamine, which slows eyeball growth. In a Guangzhou trial, adding one 40-minute outdoor class to the school day cut new cases of myopia over 3 years from 39.5% to 30.4%. In a Taiwan program that sent children outside at recess, myopia onset fell by about 60% in a year. Myopia isn't just glasses. A long eye carries a higher lifelong risk of retinal detachment, glaucoma and myopic macular damage.

How: aim for about 2 hours of daylight a day for children, with school breaks and weekends counting. Shade still works, because it's far brighter than indoors. For a child already becoming short-sighted, ask an optometrist about low-dose atropine drops or myopia-control lenses.

Keep blood sugar normal

High blood sugar damages the retina's small vessels. They leak, close off, and then grow fragile new vessels that bleed. In the landmark DCCT trial in type 1 diabetes, intensive blood-sugar control cut the risk of developing retinopathy by about three-quarters. Insulin resistance before diabetes already affects retinal blood flow. Retinopathy is silent until it's advanced, which is why yearly dilated exams matter.

How: follow the Fat Loss & Blood Sugar guide. If you have diabetes, get a dilated eye exam every year, even if you see fine. Bring a fast drop in A1c down gradually with your doctor, because a sudden fall can briefly worsen retinopathy.

Don't smoke

Smoking is the largest modifiable risk factor for macular degeneration. In a review of 17 studies, current smokers had 2–3 times the risk of never-smokers. The risk rose with how much people smoked and fell after quitting, which is the pattern of a real cause. Smoke floods the retina with oxidants, depletes its protective pigment, and narrows the blood vessels behind it. It also raises the risk of cataracts.

How: quit, including vaping as a long-term habit, since its effect on the eye isn't known. Former smokers should still avoid beta-carotene supplements.

Take the AREDS2 formula if you have intermediate AMD

The AREDS trial followed 3,640 people for about 6 years. In those with intermediate macular degeneration, a high-dose mix of vitamins C and E, beta-carotene, zinc and copper lowered the odds of progressing to advanced disease by about 25%. The follow-up trial, AREDS2, swapped beta-carotene for lutein (10 mg) and zeaxanthin (2 mg), because beta-carotene raised lung cancer in smokers and former smokers. Adding lutein and fish oil to the formula didn't slow progression any further overall. People with early drusen or healthy eyes weren't helped.

How: only take it if an eye doctor finds intermediate AMD, or advanced AMD in one eye. Choose the AREDS2 version (no beta-carotene). If you have healthy eyes, "eye vitamins" are food, not pills.

Eat egg yolks and dark greens

Lutein and zeaxanthin are yellow carotenoids that concentrate in the macula and form its pigment. They absorb blue light before it reaches the light receptors and neutralise the oxidants made there. The body can't make them, so macular pigment rises and falls with diet. In AREDS2, the people who gained the most from added lutein were those who ate the least of it. Egg yolk carries less than spinach but is absorbed far better, because it comes with its own fat. Vitamin A, from liver, egg yolk and butter, is needed separately to make the pigment that lets you see in dim light.

How: 2–3 eggs a day, plus kale, spinach, collards or arugula cooked or dressed with fat. Pastured eggs have darker yolks and more carotenoids.

Break up screen time, and blink

Close focus keeps the ciliary muscle contracted. Staring at a screen also cuts blinking roughly in half, and the blinks you do make are often incomplete, so the tear film evaporates. That causes the burning, tired, blurry feeling of "digital eye strain." Blue light from screens isn't damaging the retina. Sunlight has far more. What strains the eye is the close distance, the staring and the dry air.

How: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds, and blink fully a few times. Put the screen at arm's length and slightly below eye level. Run a humidifier in winter, and keep heater or car vents off your face. In the evening, dim and warm the light for your sleep, not your retina.

Eat wild oily fish

DHA makes up a large share of the fat in the light-sensitive outer segments of the retina's photoreceptors, which are constantly shed and rebuilt. In large cohort studies, people who eat fish regularly have less macular degeneration. The capsule trials are a different story. Fish oil didn't slow AMD in AREDS2, and in the DREAM trial it did no better than an olive-oil placebo for dry eye. The pattern is the same as for the heart: eat the fish, and don't count on the pill.

How: wild salmon, sardines, mackerel or herring 2–3 times a week. Cut seed oils too, because linoleic acid competes with DHA for space in membranes.

Use daylight, and shade the glare

Daylight is good for the eyes. It protects children from myopia, and morning light sets the clock that times sleep and alertness. The risk is heavy UV directly on the eye over decades: fishermen, skiers and outdoor workers who spend hours facing glare get more cataracts and pterygium (a fleshy growth on the white of the eye). The danger is long, unprotected glare, not daily outdoor time.

How: get outside every morning without sunglasses. For long stretches on water, snow or sand, or at high altitude, wear a brimmed hat or sunglasses that block 100% of UV. Cheap dark lenses without UV protection are worse than none, because they widen the pupil.

Worth considering

  • Warm compresses for dry eye. Most dry eye is blocked oil glands along the eyelid margin. Five minutes of a warm compress, then a gentle lid massage, melts the oil.
  • Saffron for early AMD. Small Italian trials of 20–30 mg a day found modest improvements in retinal function. They were promising but small.
  • Zinc from food. The retina holds a lot of zinc. Oysters and red meat cover it without the copper problem that comes with high-dose pills. Zinc
  • Exercise. Physically active people get less macular degeneration and glaucoma, likely through blood flow and blood sugar. Exercise

Skip, or don't count on

  • Blue-light-blocking glasses for eye strainA Cochrane review of 17 trials found no clear benefit for eye strain or for protecting the macula. Breaks and blinking do more.
  • Fish-oil capsules for dry eyeIn the DREAM trial, they did no better than an olive-oil placebo. Eat the fish instead.
  • "Eye health" multivitamins with healthy eyesThe AREDS formulas helped only people who already had intermediate AMD.
  • Beta-carotene pillsThey raised lung cancer in smokers, and AREDS2 dropped them.
  • "Throw away your glasses" eye exercisesThey can't shorten an eyeball. They may help a lazy eye or focusing problems in children, under supervision.
  • Carrots for perfect visionVitamin A prevents night blindness if you're deficient. It doesn't sharpen normal eyes.

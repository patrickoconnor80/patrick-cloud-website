Fat Loss & Blood Sugar

Health GoalFat lossInsulin resistanceBlood sugar

Body fat and high blood sugar usually come from the same problem: a body that can't clear incoming fuel quickly, fed food that's easy to overeat. Fix how well your muscles take up sugar, and eat food that fills you up, and the fat loss tends to follow. This is the short, practical version of the Metabolic Health paper.

A fat-loss week

  • Cook most meals in butter, ghee, tallow or olive oil
  • Protein at every meal: eggs, meat, fish, dairy
  • Lift twice or three times, walk daily, walk after dinner
  • Bigger breakfast and lunch, lighter dinner
  • Water, coffee, tea; drinks with alcohol on weekends at most
  • In bed for 8 hours, with morning light

Watch out

  • If you're on insulin or a sulfonylurea, changing your diet can cause low blood sugar. Tell your doctor first.
  • Losing weight very fast raises the risk of gallstones
  • Crash diets take muscle, then the fat comes back
  • If eating or weighing yourself becomes a struggle, get help

How it fits together

Every meal sends fuel into the blood, and the body has to put it somewhere. Healthy muscle takes up most of it, especially muscle that's big and has just been used. When muscle and liver are insulin resistant, insulin has to stay high to push the fuel in. High insulin keeps fat locked in fat cells, and the excess is stored as more fat, including in the liver. Two other things make it worse. Food engineered to be easy to overeat raises intake, and linoleic acid from seed oils builds up in cell membranes and fat tissue. Everything on this page either lowers the incoming load or raises the body's ability to clear it.

Weight is the result, not the lever. Pull the levers that change intake and sugar clearance, and the scale follows.

Cook real food at home

In an NIH inpatient trial, 20 adults lived on the ward for a month. For 2 weeks they ate an ultra-processed diet and for 2 weeks an unprocessed one, matched for calories, sugar, fat and fibre on the plate, and could eat as much as they liked. On the ultra-processed diet they ate about 500 calories a day more and gained about 2 lb. On the unprocessed diet they lost about the same. Restaurant, fried and packaged food is also where most seed oil comes from, and in one large cohort, French fries carried about six times the weight gain per serving of boiled or baked potato.

How: cook most meals at home in butter, ghee, tallow or olive oil. Eat out less and fried food less. Read the labels on dressings, mayo and snacks.

Eat protein at every meal

Protein is the most filling macronutrient, and the body spends more energy digesting it. In a Seattle trial, raising protein from 15% to 30% of calories while keeping carbohydrate the same made people spontaneously eat 441 fewer calories a day. Over 12 weeks they lost about 11 lb, mostly fat, without being told to eat less. In a calorie deficit, enough protein is also what keeps the weight you lose from being muscle.

How: about 0.7 g per pound of goal body weight a day (1.6 g/kg), split across meals, with 30–50 g at each. Eggs, meat, wild fish, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese.

Lift 2–3 times a week

Skeletal muscle clears most of the sugar from a meal, so more muscle means more room for it. Lifting also empties muscle glycogen, which makes muscle pull sugar in afterwards, and it raises insulin sensitivity for a day or two. When people lose weight without lifting, a large share of the loss is muscle, which lowers the calories they burn and makes regain easier. This is the clearest finding in the GLP-1 drug trials as well: lifting decides whether the weight lost is fat or muscle.

How: two or three full-body sessions a week of squats, hinges, presses and rows, pushed close to failure. Add steady cardio (zone 2) for your mitochondria.

Walk after meals

Contracting muscle takes up glucose through a route that doesn't need insulin, so walking just after eating clears meal sugar even in insulin-resistant people. A meta-analysis of crossover trials found that light walking during long sitting lowered post-meal glucose and insulin compared with sitting. Walking beat standing. The timing matters: the first hour after a meal is when the spike happens.

How: 10–15 minutes of easy walking after your biggest meals, especially dinner. Calf raises or a few flights of stairs help if you can't get outside.

Sleep 7–9 hours

In a University of Chicago trial, overweight adults followed the same reduced-calorie diet twice, once with 8.5 hours in bed and once with 5.5. They lost similar weight, but with short sleep the share that came from fat fell by 55% and muscle loss rose by 60%. They were also hungrier. One week of short sleep makes healthy young people measurably more insulin resistant.

How: follow the Sleep guide: fixed wake time, morning light, no late caffeine or alcohol.

Eat protein and vegetables first, starch last

Protein, fat and fibre eaten first slow how quickly the stomach empties and trigger the gut hormones (including GLP-1) that blunt the spike. In a Weill Cornell study, people with prediabetes ate the same meal on different days in different orders. With protein and vegetables 10 minutes before the carbohydrate, the glucose peak was over 40% lower and the overall glucose curve 39% smaller.

How: start with the meat and salad, and finish with the rice, bread or potato. If you have something sweet, have it after the meal, not on its own.

Eat carbs earlier in the day

Insulin sensitivity follows the body clock. It's highest in the morning and falls through the evening, so an identical meal gives a bigger blood-sugar spike at dinner than at breakfast. Late eating also overlaps with rising melatonin, which dampens insulin release. Carbohydrate isn't the enemy once your muscles clear it well. It's metabolically expensive to store as fat, supports thyroid conversion and fuels training, but it's better placed earlier, around activity.

How: make breakfast and lunch the bigger, carb-containing meals. Eat a lighter, protein-forward dinner 3 or more hours before bed.

Cut alcohol and liquid calories

The body can't store alcohol, so the liver burns it first and puts fat-burning on hold. Breaking it down uses up NAD⁺ and pushes the liver toward making fat, which is why heavy drinkers get fatty livers. Drinking also lowers inhibition around food and wrecks sleep. Sugary drinks and large juices add calories the body barely registers. Whole fruit is the staple, and a glass of orange juice is fine as a supplement.

How: keep alcohol to weekends at most, and stop 3 hours before bed. Water, sparkling mineral water, coffee and tea day to day.

When the basics stall: GLP-1 drugs

For people with obesity, semaglutide works. In the STEP 1 trial, adults lost 14.9% of body weight over 68 weeks against 2.4% on placebo. But the drug turns the appetite down and doesn't fix what drove the weight gain. In the extension study, people who stopped regained about two-thirds of the weight within a year, and their blood-sugar and blood-pressure gains faded with it. Without lifting and enough protein, a large share of the loss can be muscle.

How: it's a reasonable tool if you have a BMI over 30 (or over 27 with a related condition) and the basics haven't worked. While you're on it, lift 2–3 times a week, eat 0.7 g of protein per pound, and use the time to change what you eat, because that's what holds after you stop.

Worth considering

  • Cold exposure. In type 2 diabetics, about 10 days of moderate cold acclimation produced a large rise in insulin sensitivity. Cold Exposure
  • Morning light. It anchors the clock that times insulin sensitivity. Sunlight
  • Arugula and beets. Nitrate improves blood flow to working muscle. Skip antiseptic mouthwash, which blocks it. Heart: nitrate
  • Magnesium if you're low. It's needed for insulin signalling, and low magnesium is common in people with insulin resistance. Magnesium
  • A continuous glucose monitor for 2 weeks. Eat the same meal at 8 am and 8 pm, or with and without a walk afterwards, and see the difference yourself.

Skip, or don't count on

  • Crash diets and juice cleansesFast loss on very little protein takes muscle, slows your metabolism and makes regain likely.
  • "Fat burner" pills and detox teasMostly caffeine, laxatives and diuretics. You lose water, not fat.
  • Treating fasting windows as magicIn a 12-week trial, eating within an 8-hour window lost no more weight than three meals a day, and more of what was lost was muscle. Earlier eating helps. A late, narrow window doesn't.
  • Cardio instead of liftingCardio is good for you, but on its own it doesn't protect muscle while you diet.
  • Apple cider vinegar shotsThe blood-sugar effect is small, and the acid erodes your teeth. Teeth
  • "Low-fat" packaged foodsThe fat is often replaced with sugar and starch, and the oil that's left is seed oil.

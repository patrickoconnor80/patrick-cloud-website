Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin)

VitaminFoundational

Protects your nerves and builds red blood cells. Deficiency damage can be permanent.

At a glance

Essential · test if at risk

  • Builds red blood cells Without it, cells come out large and dysfunctional. why ↓
  • Keeps homocysteine down Recycles homocysteine back into methionine. why ↓

Mechanism

Two enzyme roles: methionine synthase (converts homocysteine back to methionine — see Methionine and Vitamin B9) and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (converts to succinyl-CoA, feeding the TCA cycle).

The mutase is the "fats and amino acids" role: it is the last step in breaking down odd-chain fatty acids, cholesterol's side chain, and the amino acids valine, isoleucine, methionine and threonine, all of which funnel through propionyl-CoA → methylmalonyl-CoA. When B12 is short, methylmalonic acid backs up, which is why MMA is the most specific blood marker of deficiency. Calling this "energy production" is a stretch: it is a minor fuel route, and extra B12 does nothing for energy unless you are deficient. Any fatigue comes from the anemia.

The red-cell effect runs through folate. Methionine synthase is the only enzyme that takes the methyl group off 5-methyl-THF, so without B12, folate gets stuck in that form (the "methyl-folate trap"). Dividing marrow cells can't make enough thymidine for DNA, so they grow but don't divide, giving large, fragile red cells (megaloblastic anemia). High-dose folic acid can fix the anemia while the nerve damage keeps progressing, which is why B12 should be checked before treating macrocytic anemia with folate alone.

The myelin effect is less settled. The leading explanations are low S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) starving myelin-protein methylation, and methylmalonyl-CoA buildup putting abnormal branched and odd-chain fatty acids into myelin lipids. Either way, deficiency produces subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord and peripheral neuropathy.

Intake / absorption

RDA roughly 2.4 mcg/day for adults. Comes from animal or fortified foods; absorption requires intrinsic factor and an intact ileum, and decreases with age generally. Higher deficiency risk with a vegan diet, pernicious anemia, gastric surgery/disease, or metformin use.

Deficiency effects

Megaloblastic anemia; neuropathy, numbness/tingling, gait issues, cognitive/mood changes; elevated MMA and homocysteine. Neuropathy and cognitive changes can be irreversible if deficiency goes uncorrected too long.

Excess effects

Generally low toxicity.

Market Status

Growth & AI — The November Drawdown

QQQ is one bad day from a bear market after its worst week since March 2020. Nvidia lost its founder-CEO and now faces a reported SEC accounting probe, inflation is running hot, and the Fed is about to raise rates again. Meanwhile the AI leaders trade at lower multiples than Walmart and Costco. Is this a bubble deflating, or growth stocks priced like value stocks?

The week of Nov 16

Friday, Nov 20 was the worst day of the drawdown. QQQ fell 5.7% to $608.45, the S&P 500 fell 3.7%, and the Dow fell 1.9%. The S&P 500 and the Dow both closed in a correction, 10%+ below their records, and the Nasdaq Composite gave back all of its 2026 gains. QQQ lost 10.5% on the week, the biggest weekly drop since March 2020, and it now sits 19.9% below its Oct 6 peak, a fraction of a percent from the 20% line that defines a bear market. Chips took the worst of it: SMH fell 10.6%, Nvidia fell 14% to $125.53 (its seventh straight loss, and about 23% for the week), and AMD and Micron each fell more than 13%. The high-beta ETF SPHB fell 7.1%. The VIX closed at 35.9. Treasuries rallied as money moved into bonds: the 10-year yield closed near 4.9%, after peaking above 5.1% on Nov 13. My market-cycle score on the Data page closed at −91.5, Washout, deep in the zone where high beta has historically bounced over the next three months, but in 2022 it also stayed there through repeated legs down.

The setup

QQQ peaked at $759.66 on Oct 6 and closed at $656.95 on Nov 17, down 13.5%, after a four-session losing streak. The S&P 500 is at a five-month low, and the Nasdaq-100 is on track for its worst November since 2008. This is the third 10%+ drop from a record high in 2026 (March, July, and now). Only 2000 and 2018 had three in one year. The two years ended very differently: 2000 kept falling toward an 83% loss, while 2018 bottomed at −23% in December and was back at a record by April 2019. Across every 10% drop since 1999, QQQ was higher a year later in 9 of 14 cases, and the misses were all in 2000 or 2022. The damage is concentrated in semis, neoclouds, and the speculative AI names: Nvidia is down about 30% from its high, and data-center operators like IREN, Applied Digital, and Cipher fell more than 14% on Nov 5 alone.

The rare pattern

QQQ fell 6.5% on Nov 5 and recovered almost all of it within four sessions. That sequence (a 5%+ drop, then three sessions with 1%+ gains and no 1% down days) has happened only four times since QQQ launched in 1999: twice in 2000 and twice in 2001. All four came before drawdowns of at least 59%. That's the scariest chart going around. It's also four data points from a single bubble, when QQQ traded at about 82x earnings. It trades at about 28x trailing today. A pattern with that small a sample says more about volatility than about what happens next.

The momentum crash

The more telling chart is Morgan Stanley's U.S. TMT Momentum Factor, which as of Oct 25 had fallen 62% from its highs, before the Nov 5 and Nov 12 shocks. It had also logged 65 daily moves larger than 5% this year, close to the 70 it recorded across the whole 1999–2001 boom and bust (per a widely shared post citing Morgan Stanley; I haven't seen the underlying data). A momentum factor is a long/short basket: it owns recent winners and shorts recent losers. A 62% drop means the crowded AI winners fell far harder than everything else, not that tech fell 62%. That's how QQQ can be down only about 10% while IONQ, NBIS, and the neoclouds are down 50–65% from their peaks. It also gives the index-level drawdown its context: the forced selling was concentrated in the crowded trade.

What usually follows

Momentum crashes this violent tend to happen near turning points, not in the middle of trends: 2009 and early 2022 are the usual comparisons. Much of the crowding has already been flushed out, which supports the case that the forced selling is mostly done. The catch is who leads afterward. The first rebound is often led by the heavily shorted laggards, not the old winners, and leadership can rotate for good. Many 2021 momentum names never got back to their highs, while Nvidia and Meta led the next cycle. A momentum unwind can mark the bottom of the selling without telling you which names come back. For speculative names, that still depends on each company's own milestones.

02

What Caused It

Five separate shocks in three weeks. Most of them hit the AI trade specifically. Rates hit every long-duration growth stock.

1 · The midterms (Nov 5)

A Democratic sweep set off fears that Congress and the states would restrict AI and data-center construction. Some governors-elect campaigned on pausing new data centers. The Nasdaq fell 5.5%, wiping out about $2–2.5T. These are fears about future policy, not enacted rules, and that's why most of the drop reversed within days.

2 · The financing chain (Nov 6 onward)

This is the most substantive of the three. ING is reportedly trying to sell about $500M of data-center loans, part of a wider move by banks to offload data-center credit risk. Hyperscalers are issuing debt to fund capex. Nebius raised $5.75B of debt in Q3 alone and guided to $40–45B of capex for 2027. Nobody significant is defaulting. The worry is the chain: neoclouds borrow against GPU contracts, the contracts depend on AI labs keeping their spending going, and the labs depend on capital markets staying open.

3 · Nvidia loses its CEO (Nov 12)

Jensen Huang resigned as CEO and director after reports of extramarital affairs, one of them with a woman described as a suspected Chinese intelligence asset who also worked with Megaspeed, a Singapore customer the DOJ is investigating for diverting restricted chips to China. CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO. NVDA fell as much as 14% intraday and closed down 4.3%. At least ten analysts downgraded it, and Bernstein halved its target to $200.

4 · Hot inflation and a rate hike (Nov 10 onward)

October CPI rose 0.4% (0.3% expected) and core rose 0.3% (0.2% expected), the third hotter-than-expected month in a row, driven by shelter, food, medical care, and insurance. No year-over-year figure was published because of a data gap from the 2025 shutdown, but core is estimated near 2.8%, up from 2.6%. The Fed is already tightening: it raised rates in September to 3.75–4.00%, and markets now price about a 95% chance of another quarter-point hike on Dec 9, with the rest on a half point. Waller has floated 50 basis points, Logan sees two more hikes, and Williams said AI investment itself could argue for more hikes next year. The 10-year yield is back above 5%. This is the 2022 mechanism: higher discount rates hit long-duration growth stocks hardest, regardless of their fundamentals.

5 · An SEC probe and a record IPO (Nov 19–20)

Anthropic listed on the Nasdaq as ANT on Nov 19, raising $81.3B at $83 a share, the largest IPO ever. It opened 34% higher and closed its first day up 17%, but the rest of the AI trade sold off around it, and ANT itself fell roughly 8–11% on Friday, back toward its IPO price. A record raise pulls money out of the existing AI names, and many people read a mega-IPO as a sign of a top. Then on Nov 20 Reuters reported that SEC enforcement staff are looking at possible accounting issues at Nvidia, citing two people briefed on the matter. There's no formal allegation, and the SEC hasn't confirmed the scope. But it landed on a company with no permanent CEO and an open national-security investigation, and NVDA went from down about 6% to down 14% that afternoon. Three days after a strong earnings report, the stock is 19% below its pre-earnings close, so the market is no longer trading Nvidia on its orders.

Growth Priced Below the Grocery Store

The strongest point in the bull case: the AI leaders now trade at lower forward multiples than the defensive staples that usually anchor portfolios in a sell-off.

Some sources put Walmart and Costco at 44x and 41x.

How cheap, historically

At or below the 2022 bear-market lows, depending on the measure. Bloomberg data via TheStreet has the Nasdaq-100's forward P/E at 18x for the first time since the Covid crash in March 2020, after a 25% de-rating this year. A separate read of FactSet's weekly data puts the tech sector at its cheapest since January 2023, the bottom of the 2022 bear market. After the week of Nov 16, with QQQ down another 7% from Nov 17 and Nvidia down another 19%, both are cheaper still: roughly 17x for the index, with Nvidia well below the ~13x shown in the chart. Nvidia's forward multiple has briefly dropped below Kimberly-Clark's, a toilet-paper company. The trailing numbers show the same thing: on Nov 5, seven of nine top AI stocks had lower trailing P/Es than Costco (51x) and Walmart (46x), while growing revenue several times faster.

The catch: cyclical P/Es lie

A low forward P/E is only cheap if the “E” holds up. Micron at ~9x trailing on 379% revenue growth is the classic example: the market prices memory as a cycle, and it puts the low multiple at the peak of earnings. Nvidia at ~13x forward is the market saying the same thing about AI capex: it doubts fiscal 2028 earnings will look like fiscal 2027's. Walmart at 38x is the market paying for earnings it's confident about. So the gap isn't a mispricing you can just collect. It's a bet on whether AI capex is a durable new spending layer or a cycle that peaks. That said, the gap is the widest it has been in this cycle, and starting multiples this low have historically been good entry points for anyone with a multi-year horizon.

04

Is It a Bubble?

The bubble case rests on two things failing together: AI demand proving weaker than expected, and the debt financing the buildout going bad. They're separate questions with different answers.

Is the demand real?

Yes, and the evidence is in revenue

This isn't 2000, when the leaders were unprofitable and QQQ traded at 82x. Nvidia guided its October quarter to $108B in revenue. Nebius's run-rate went from $3.0B to $6.4B in one quarter, and it says it's supply-constrained. Anthropic is going public near a $1T valuation, and the IPO is reportedly heavily oversubscribed. IonQ says Superion 256 is sold out for 2027. Adoption is also early: enterprises are only starting to deploy agents, inference demand grows with usage rather than with one-off training runs, and robotics hasn't started buying compute at scale.

Can the financing outrun it?

Yes, and that's the actual risk

Real demand and overbuilt capacity aren't mutually exclusive. Railroads in the 1840s and fiber in the late 1990s were both real technologies whose buildouts were financed faster than revenue arrived. The internet worked, and Global Crossing still went bankrupt. The thing to watch isn't whether AI is useful. It's whether capex funded by debt (Nebius's $40–45B 2027 plan, hyperscaler bond issuance, banks offloading data-center loans) runs ahead of what end customers pay for. If that gap shows up, the leveraged middle layer (neoclouds, colocation, smaller data-center operators) breaks first, and the selling spreads up to the chipmakers.

My read

This looks more like a valuation reset inside a real buildout than a 2000-style top. The leaders are profitable, multiples have already compressed 25%, and the drop was driven by policy fear and one CEO scandal, not a collapse in orders. What would change my mind is the credit side failing: a neocloud missing a debt payment, a large AI lab cutting compute commitments, or hyperscalers guiding capex down. None of those has happened. In fact, Nvidia's Nov 17 report pointed the other way. The new risk is the SEC report: if it turns into a restatement of revenue, the cheap forward multiple was built on numbers that weren't real, and the valuation case above falls apart for Nvidia specifically. Until there's more than an anonymously sourced report, I'm treating it as an overhang, not a finding. The risk that has shown up is the other one: the 2022 scenario, where growth stocks fall because rates rise, even with fine fundamentals. With the Fed hiking and the 10-year above 5%, the comparison with 2022 is stronger than it looked two weeks ago. Two differences remain. In 2022, rates went from zero to 4%+ in a year, while today's hikes are quarter points from 4%. And the Nasdaq-100 entered 2022 near 30x forward earnings, while it's now near 18x. A 2022-sized drop from here would need earnings estimates to fall, not just multiples. The Dec 9 decision, and especially the Dec 10 November CPI, will show whether the Fed is close to finished or just getting started.

What beta means for the watchlist

On Nov 13, QQQ fell about 2% and IonQ fell 6.4%. By Nov 17 IonQ had fallen six days straight, about 30%, to a one-year closing low of $24.84, and it traded near $23.50 on Nov 18. That's the ratio to plan around: the speculative names on my list (IONQ, ONDS, NBIS, DNA) move two to four times the index in both directions. If the bottom is close, they'll rebound hardest. If it isn't, they'll fall hardest. With beta like that, position size matters more than how confident I am. Nov 20 showed it again. QQQ fell 5.7%, and NBIS fell 16%, ONDS 11%, DNA 9%, and IONQ 9% to $20.72. NBIS is now down 55% from its high a month ago.

05

The Nvidia Leadership Question

Can shareholders reinstate him?

Not directly. Shareholders don't vote on who is CEO. The board decides, and Huang owns only about 3.6% of Nvidia with no super-voting shares, unlike Zuckerberg at Meta. He also resigned from the board. What exists is pressure: a reinstatement petition with 135,000+ signatures (Musk signed it, and it claims thousands of Nvidia employees), a letter from Altimeter's Brad Gerstner and Atreides' Gavin Baker asking the board to consider “the potential return of Jensen Huang” through a slow, deliberate process, and the Kobeissi Letter calling for a special meeting with an advisory vote. A return is possible, but it would be a board decision after its investigation, not a shareholder vote.

Legal and regulatory exposure

No charges have been reported, and an affair isn't a crime. The risk isn't the affair. It's the national-security overlap: the DOJ's Megaspeed chip-diversion probe, a House Intelligence Committee letter, Huang being asked to testify, and Commerce Secretary Lutnick due before the Senate Banking Committee on Dec 10. Nvidia says it has no reason to believe secrets were compromised, and the board has hired Wachtell Lipton. Investigations like this take months. Until they finish, the board can't credibly bring him back, so expect the overhang to last through at least December.

Does it change the business?

Near term, very little. Kress has been CFO for 13 years, the roadmap is set through Rubin, and customers buy Nvidia because nothing else matches it. Long term, Huang was the strategist who saw CUDA and AI years early, and that's hard to replace. Musk says “critical deals will be lost” without him. The Nov 17 earnings call, Kress's first as interim CEO, was the first real test.

The first print without Huang

It passed. Fiscal Q3 revenue was $115.8B (+103%) against a $108B guide and ~$109B consensus, and data center revenue was $107.6B (+110%). Q4 is guided to $130B ±3% against ~$125B expected, assuming zero China data-center revenue, and fiscal 2028 growth was raised to 75% from 70%. The soft spot is gross margin, guided down to 71–72% as the Vera Rubin ramp starts. The stock rose 3–4% after hours, from about $155. That's the clearest evidence yet against the “AI demand isn't real” case: the market isn't selling Nvidia because orders are falling.

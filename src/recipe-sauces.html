Sauces & Seasonings

CondimentsDipsSpice blendNo seed oils

Eleven sauces and a taco seasoning that replace the store-bought versions. The biggest difference is in the mayo.

From Cooking with Chris: 11 sauces and taco seasoning. Where he gives no amount, I filled one in and said so. The nutrition numbers and the notes at the bottom are mine.

At a glance

Make the mayo ones first

LA = linoleic acid (omega-6). The three mayo sauces use avocado oil mayo. The same sauce made with regular soybean-oil mayo has about 4× the LA. Why ↓

Good to know

  • Buy avocado oil mayo, or make it
  • Pico, salsa, guacamole and chimichurri are just vegetables, oil and salt
  • Taco seasoning costs pennies and has no fillers
why ↓

Watch out

  • Honey sriracha and honey mustard are mostly sugar
  • HFCS isn't worse than the honey that replaces it
  • Check that your avocado oil mayo is actually avocado oil

2Garlic & chili aioli

Serving: 2 tbsp

  • 2garlic cloves (or more)
  • ½ tspsmoked paprika
  • pinchsalt
  • 1 cupavocado oil mayo
  • 1–2 tbspsherry vinegar
  • 1 tsptomato paste
  • ¼ tspground chipotle
  • pinchcayenne

Pound the garlic, smoked paprika and a pinch of salt to a paste in a mortar (or a food processor). Stir in the mayo, vinegar, tomato paste, chipotle and cayenne. Cover and refrigerate. Best with potatoes, and great with a burger.

Red wine vinegar stands in for sherry vinegar, and cayenne for chipotle powder, in the numbers.

9Guacamole

Serving: ¼ cup

  • 5ripe avocados (4–6)
  • ⅓ cupwhite onion
  • 2 tbspserrano or jalapeño
  • ¼–½ cupcilantro
  • juice of 1lime (or more)
  • to tastecoarse salt

In a mortar, mash the onion, chili, half the cilantro and a big pinch of coarse salt to release the flavor. Add the avocados, lime juice and the rest of the cilantro, then mash to the texture you like and salt to taste. Serve with eggs and sourdough, Mexican food, or seed-oil-free chips.

The 1 tsp of salt is mine. Serrano is counted as jalapeño.

11Fire-roasted salsa

Serving: ¼ cup

  • 1 pinttomatoes
  • 1yellow onion
  • 8garlic cloves, unpeeled
  • 3jalapeños
  • ⅓ cupcilantro
  • juice of 1lime
  • ¼ tspground cumin
  • pinchcayenne
  • pinchoregano
  • to tastesalt & pepper
  • drizzleolive oil

Toss the tomatoes, unpeeled garlic, onion and jalapeños in a little oil on a sheet pan. Broil on high until charred and blistered, then flip and char the other side. Peel the garlic, then blend everything with the cilantro, lime and spices to the texture you like. Salt to taste. It's better after a few hours in the fridge.

The amounts of salt and oil are mine.

12Taco seasoning

Serving: 4 oz cooked beef

  • 4 tspchili powder
  • 3 tspcumin
  • 1 tsppaprika
  • 1 tspcrushed red pepper
  • 1 tspsalt
  • ½ tspgarlic powder
  • ½ tsponion powder
  • ½ tsporegano
  • ½ tspblack pepper

Mix. This seasons about 2 lb of meat, so halve it for 1 lb, or scale it up and keep a jar. It's cheaper than store packets and has no fillers. Chris uses Simply Organic spices.

The nutrition is for 4 oz of cooked 85% lean ground beef plus its share of the seasoning (2 lb raw ≈ 24 oz cooked).

Nutrition notes

The thread's opening claim: store sauces are "loaded with seed oils, high fructose corn syrup, thickeners."

ClaimWhat the evidence showsHolds?
Store sauces are full of seed oilsTrue for mayo and everything built on it. Regular mayo is soybean oil. More ↓Yes
…and high fructose corn syrupTrue for regular US ketchup, but HFCS is about as bad as table sugar or honey, not worse. More ↓Half

Mayo is where the seed oil is

USDA's standard mayonnaise is made with soybean oil, and 39% of its weight is linoleic acid. A 2-tablespoon serving carries about 11 g, more than most people need in a day by the Linoleic Acid paper's estimate. Avocado oil mayo has about a quarter of that, roughly 3 g per 2 tablespoons. Avocado oil is mostly oleic acid, the same fat as olive oil. Mayo is also the base of aioli, burger sauce, sriracha mayo, tartar sauce and most creamy dressings, so this one swap covers a lot of the omega-6 that comes from condiments.

Two caveats. First, the swap only works if the bottle really is avocado oil. In UC Davis testing, most US avocado oils were rancid or cut with other oils, some with soybean oil (details on the marinades page). Second, avocado oil mayo is still about 80% fat, about 100 kcal per tablespoon. The improvement is in the type of fat, not the amount. (Strong: USDA composition data. The avocado mayo figure is estimated from its ingredients.)

HFCS: right about the label, wrong about the risk

Regular US ketchup is sweetened with high fructose corn syrup, so his burger sauce, made with regular ketchup, still has it. But HFCS (42–55% fructose) is chemically close to table sugar (50% fructose) and honey (about 38% fructose and 31% glucose by weight). A 2018 meta-analysis of controlled trials found no difference between HFCS and sucrose on CRP, the main inflammation marker, and no difference between fructose and glucose.[1] The evidence was rated low quality. One author sits on a carbohydrate-industry consortium and an ILSI Europe expert group.

So the useful question is how much sugar, not which kind. The honey sriracha is about half honey by weight, and the honey mustard and stir fry sauce aren't far behind. They're good sauces, but count them as sugar. (Moderate: trials agree, but they're short and few)

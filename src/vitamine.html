Vitamin E (Alpha-tocopherol)

VitaminUseful

The antioxidant that protects the fats in your cell membranes.

At a glance

Essential
  • Protects cell membranes Stops chain reactions that oxidize membrane fats. why ↓
  • Supports immunity Needed for normal immune cell function. why ↓
Strong human evidenceSome human evidence

How to take it

  • 15 mg a day
  • Avocado, spinach, wild salmon and trout, olive oil
  • The less linoleic acid you eat, the less vitamin E you need

Watch out

  • High doses can increase bleeding risk, especially alongside vitamin K

Signs you're low

  • Nerve problems, poor coordination (rare, mostly with fat malabsorption)

Mechanism

Scavenges lipid peroxyl radicals, limiting lipid peroxidation — vitamin C regenerates vitamin E after it's used this way (see Vitamin C). This is also the mechanistic reason membrane PUFA content matters: more oxidizable substrate raises the antioxidant demand on vitamin E, which connects directly to the hub paper's linoleic acid argument.

↑ back to summary

Intake

RDA 15 mg/day alpha-tocopherol for adults. Upper limit 1,000 mg/day for supplemental alpha-tocopherol under the U.S. DRI framework.

↑ back to summary

Deficiency effects

Rare; can occur with fat malabsorption — peripheral neuropathy, ataxia, hemolytic anemia, immune impairment.

↑ back to summary

Excess effects

High doses may increase bleeding risk via a vitamin K interaction — worth noting alongside Vitamin K1 and Vitamin K2 if combining supplements.

↑ back to summary

Top foods

  • Avocado
  • Spinach
  • Wild salmon and trout
  • Olive oil
  • Red palm oil (also rich in tocotrienols)

Seed oils and nuts, which top most vitamin E lists, are left off on purpose: they are also the richest sources of linoleic acid, and the extra PUFA they bring raises the demand on vitamin E that they supply (see the Mechanism section above and Linoleic Acid).

Skin Health

Health GoalCollagenSun damageBarrier

Skin is three jobs stacked on top of each other: a waterproof barrier on the surface, a collagen scaffold underneath, and defences against what sunlight does to both. Each one runs on specific nutrients, and most of them come from food.

A skin week

  • Beef liver once (about 3 oz), plus eggs and butter
  • Oysters once, wild salmon or sardines 2–3 times
  • 2½–3½ tbsp tomato paste a day, cooked into food with olive oil
  • 2 tbsp collagen or a mug of bone broth, with a vitamin C food
  • Sun most days, building up slowly, stopping before you go pink
  • Cook with tallow, butter or olive oil

Watch out

  • Don't eat liver every day. Retinol builds up, and the upper limit is 3,000 mcg a day. Avoid high-dose retinol if pregnant.
  • Skip beta-carotene pills. They raised lung cancer in smokers.
  • Biotin megadoses skew blood tests, including thyroid and heart tests
  • Zinc above 40 mg a day drains copper

How it fits together

The surface of skin is a wall of flattened cells held together by fats, mostly ceramides. That wall keeps water in and germs out. Under it, the dermis is a mesh of collagen and elastin fibres that gives skin its firmness and spring. Sunlight hits both. Some of it is good for you (vitamin D, nitric oxide), and some of it oxidises fats and breaks down collagen. Aged, wrinkled, slow-healing skin is what happens when the damage outruns the repair.

Protein, steady blood sugar and sun without burning support all three

Wrinkles, sagging, sun spots and slow healing: damage outrunning repair

The same sun does more harm to skin that's low on repair nutrients and loaded with oxidisable fat.

Get sun, but never burn

Most visible skin ageing on the face and hands is sun damage: collagen broken down by UV-triggered enzymes, and DNA damage that becomes spots and, later, skin cancers. Burning is the clearest marker that the dose was too high. But sun also makes vitamin D and releases nitric oxide from the skin, and people who avoid it entirely do worse on heart outcomes. So the goal is regular, gradual exposure, not none.

Skin adapts. A few weeks of short, regular sessions in spring thickens the outer layer and builds a tan, which together raise the dose it takes to burn. The diet levers below raise it further.

How: start with 10–15 minutes around midday and build up as you tan. Stop before you go pink. For long days out, use shade, a hat and clothing, and sunscreen on your face and hands, which take the most lifetime sun.

Eat real vitamin A

Cells turn retinol into retinoic acid, which switches on the genes that set how fast skin cells divide, mature and shed. Low vitamin A gives dry, rough, bumpy skin. Tretinoin and isotretinoin, the strongest prescription treatments for wrinkles and acne, are retinoic acid itself, given at doses far above what food supplies. Food keeps the system stocked. The drugs push it hard.

Plants don't contain vitamin A. They contain beta-carotene, which some people convert well and many convert poorly. Retinol from animal foods skips that step.

How: 700–900 mcg a day. About 3 oz of beef liver once a week covers most of it, and eggs, butter and cheese fill in the rest. Liver every day is too much.

Feed the collagen machine

Collagen is about one-third glycine, with lots of proline. To fold it into a stable triple helix, the enzymes that finish it need vitamin C. Without vitamin C the collagen falls apart, which is scurvy. Copper then runs lysyl oxidase, the enzyme that cross-links finished fibres into a strong mesh. Muscle meat is rich in methionine and low in glycine, so a diet without skin, bones or broth runs short on the main raw material.

Collagen supplements look good in pooled trials: better hydration, elasticity and wrinkle depth after about 90 days. But a 2025 meta-analysis of 23 trials split them by funder. The benefit came from trials paid for by collagen makers, and the independent trials found no effect. The building-block argument still holds. The face-cream promises are weaker than the marketing.

How: about 10 g of glycine a day from 2 tbsp collagen, 2½ tsp glycine powder, a mug of bone broth, or skin-on and bone-in cuts. Pair it with a vitamin C food such as red pepper, kiwi or orange. Get copper from liver and oysters.

Eat tomato paste with olive oil

Lycopene, the red pigment in tomatoes, collects in skin and soaks up the reactive oxygen that UV creates. Two small randomised trials tested it as food. In one, 40 g of tomato paste a day with olive oil cut UV reddening after 10 weeks. In the other, 55 g a day for 12 weeks cut reddening and protected procollagen and mitochondrial DNA in skin biopsies. Cooking and fat both raise how much lycopene you absorb, so paste beats raw tomatoes.

It's a modest, slow effect, like a low-SPF layer built from the inside. It doesn't replace not burning.

How: 2½–3½ tbsp of tomato paste a day (40–55 g), cooked into sauces, chili or soup with olive oil. Give it 10–12 weeks.

Eat wild oily fish

EPA goes into skin cell membranes and dampens the inflammatory signalling UV sets off. In a Manchester trial, people who took 4 g of EPA a day for three months needed a bigger UV dose to burn, and showed less UV-induced p53, a marker of DNA damage. That's a high dose, more than fish alone supplies, but it shows the direction. Wild fish also brings vitamin D, selenium and astaxanthin, the red pigment in salmon.

How: wild salmon (about 8 oz) or a can or two of sardines, 2–3 times a week.

Cut seed oils

Linoleic acid is the main polyunsaturated fat in skin, and it oxidises easily under UV. Skin does need a little of it for the ceramides in its barrier, but deficiency is very rare and takes almost none. What's in question is the excess. A Western diet built on soybean and canola oil loads skin with far more than the barrier uses.

In hairless mice, diets rich in polyunsaturated oil raised UV-induced skin cancer compared with saturated fat. The one human diet trial cut total fat, not linoleic acid in particular: 76 people with prior skin cancer went to 20% of calories from fat and grew fewer new pre-cancerous spots (actinic keratoses) over two years. That fits the idea but doesn't isolate it. No trial has cut linoleic acid alone and measured sunburn.

How: cook with tallow, butter, ghee or olive oil, and skip fried restaurant food and packaged snacks made with seed oils. Skin fat turns over slowly, so allow months.

Keep blood sugar down

Glucose sticks to proteins without an enzyme and forms advanced glycation end-products (AGEs). Collagen is long-lived, so it collects them. AGEs cross-link collagen fibres the wrong way, making them stiff and brittle, and the body's enzymes struggle to clear them. People with diabetes show this in their skin, and higher blood sugar tracks with looking older in cohort studies. It's the same insulin-resistance problem that drives heart disease, showing up in a different tissue.

How: the metabolic basics. Lift, cut refined flour and sugary drinks, and keep starch around training. Browned, charred food adds some AGEs too, but the blood-sugar side is the bigger one.

Get enough zinc

About 5% of the body's zinc is in skin. Every step of repair uses it: cells dividing to fill a gap, enzymes clearing old scaffolding, and immune cells keeping the wound clean. Zinc deficiency shows up as slow healing, rashes and hair loss, and the genetic form causes raw, crusted skin that clears with zinc. Zinc has also been tested for acne, with modest benefit.

How: 8–11 mg a day. Six oysters once a week covers it several times over, and beef fills in. If you take a zinc supplement long-term, keep it under 40 mg and take a little copper with it.

Worth considering

  • Nicotinamide, if you've had skin cancer. In the ONTRAC trial, 500 mg twice a day for a year cut new non-melanoma skin cancers by 23%. It feeds DNA repair after UV. Use nicotinamide, not niacin, which causes flushing. Vitamin B3
  • Topical tretinoin. The best-tested cream for sun-damaged skin. It's prescription-only and irritating at first. It's vitamin A's own pathway at a high dose. Vitamin A
  • Creatine cream. Raised collagen production in skin cells, and one trial found firmer skin. That trial was run by the company that sells the cream. Creatine
  • Fewer high-glycemic carbs and less skim milk, for acne. A low-glycemic diet cut acne lesions in a randomised trial, and milk, skim milk most of all, tracks with acne in cohorts. Insulin and IGF-1 drive oil glands.

Skip, or don't count on

  • Biotin for skinIt only helps if you're deficient, which is rare. Megadoses throw off blood tests, including troponin and thyroid tests. Biotin
  • Beta-carotene pillsHigh doses slightly raise the burn threshold, but they raised lung cancer and deaths in smokers. Get carotenoids from food.
  • Collagen as a wrinkle cureFine as a source of glycine. The independent trials don't show it smoothing wrinkles.
  • Antioxidant serums and pills in place of not burningNothing you swallow or apply undoes a burn. Diet raises the threshold a little. Dose is still what matters.

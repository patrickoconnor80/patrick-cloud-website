The Research

Mechanisms, studies and evidence grades for each claim above.

Eases anxiety

German chamomile flowers contain the flavone apigenin (mostly as apigenin-7-glucoside), along with the essential-oil sesquiterpenes α-bisabolol and chamazulene. In 1995 researchers showed that apigenin binds the central benzodiazepine site of the GABAA receptor and produces anxiolytic effects in mice without sedating them or impairing their muscle control.1 It's a weak partial ligand — much gentler than a drug like diazepam, which fits the size of the human effects. (Animal — receptor pharmacology)

The human trials come from one University of Pennsylvania group (Amsterdam, Mao):

  • Acute: in 57 people with mild-to-moderate generalized anxiety disorder, a chamomile extract standardized to 1.2% apigenin (220–1,100 mg/day, 8 weeks) reduced Hamilton Anxiety scores more than placebo (p = 0.047). Every secondary outcome moved the same way.2
  • Long-term: 179 people with moderate-to-severe GAD took 1,500 mg/day open-label for 12 weeks, and 93 responders were then randomized to continue or switch to placebo. Relapse was lower on chamomile (15% vs 26%), but not significantly. Symptoms stayed significantly lower, and body weight and blood pressure also fell.3

Overall, a modest, real anti-anxiety effect with essentially no side effects — more reliable than chamomile's sleep reputation. (Moderate — two RCTs, one group, NIH-funded) For a stronger herb acting on the same receptor, see Chinese Skullcap, whose baicalin and wogonin are also flavone ligands at the GABAA benzodiazepine site.

Better sleep quality

The sleep trials split by population:

  • Older adults: in 60 day-care nursing-home attendees in Iran, 200 mg chamomile extract twice daily for 4 weeks improved Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores compared with flour-capsule placebo (single-blind).4
  • Postpartum women: in 80 Taiwanese women with poor sleep after giving birth, a daily cup of chamomile tea for 2 weeks improved physical-symptom-related sleep problems and depression scores. The benefit was gone 4 weeks after they stopped.5
  • Chronic primary insomnia: in a 28-day pilot RCT in 34 adults, 270 mg extract twice daily didn't significantly change any sleep-diary measure. There was a moderate trend toward faster sleep onset and fewer awakenings, but total sleep time trended toward placebo.6

Chamomile helps people whose sleep is disturbed by stress, age or circumstance more than people with established insomnia. Much of the effect is probably the anxiolytic effect above plus a calming bedtime routine. (Moderate — small, mostly unblinded or open-label trials; the one proper insomnia RCT was null)

Lower blood sugar in type 2 diabetes

In an 8-week trial in 64 Iranian adults with type 2 diabetes, 3 cups a day of chamomile tea (3 g in 150 mL hot water, drunk right after meals) lowered HbA1c, serum insulin, HOMA-IR, total cholesterol, triglycerides and LDL compared with a water control.7 A companion paper from the same trial reported better antioxidant status. Proposed mechanisms include α-glucosidase inhibition and aldose-reductase inhibition by chamomile flavonoids. (Moderate — one small trial, water control rather than placebo tea, same group reporting both papers)

How to take it

  • Tea: 2–3 g of dried flowers (about 1–2 teabags, or a heaped tablespoon of loose heads) per cup. Cover it while steeping so the volatile oils stay in the cup, and steep 5–10 minutes.
  • Extract (anxiety): standardized to ~1.2% apigenin, 500–1,500 mg/day in divided doses, as in the Penn trials. A cup of tea gives only a few milligrams of apigenin, well below that.
  • Type: German chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla / recutita) is the one used in the trials. Roman chamomile (Chamaemelum nobile) is a different plant.
  • Quality: whole, intact flower heads keep their apigenin and oils much better than finely ground teabag dust.
  • Evening pairing: with L-theanine and magnesium — different mechanisms, no known conflicts.

Safety

Cautions: chamomile is in the Asteraceae (daisy) family. People allergic to ragweed, chrysanthemum, marigold or milk thistle can react, rarely with anaphylaxis. Coumarin constituents and case reports of increased INR mean anyone on warfarin should be careful with large, regular amounts. Chamomile inhibits iron absorption far less than black tea (47% versus 79–94% in the radio-iron study on the Assam page), but it's still significant.

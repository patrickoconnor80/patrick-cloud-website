Thyroid & Cold Tolerance

Health GoalIodineSeleniumMetabolic rate

Thyroid hormone sets how fast every cell burns fuel and makes heat. Cold hands, low energy, constipation and thinning hair are the classic signs it's running low. Making it takes iodine and iron. Activating it takes selenium and enough food. Stress and dieting turn it down.

A thyroid week

  • Salt food with iodized salt, or eat cod, shrimp, dairy and eggs most days
  • Wild fish or sardines 2–3 times, eggs daily (selenium)
  • Red meat or oysters a few times (iron, zinc)
  • Liver once (vitamin A)
  • Three real meals. Don't stack long fasts on hard training.
  • A cool morning walk in light clothes, building up over weeks

Watch out

  • Too much iodine can trigger thyroid problems, especially in Hashimoto's. Skip kelp pills and high-dose iodine.
  • Stop biotin 2–3 days before a thyroid blood test. It skews the result.
  • Don't change a thyroid prescription on your own
  • Take thyroid medication away from iron, calcium, coffee and fiber

How it fits together

The brain releases TSH, which tells the thyroid to grab iodine from the blood and attach it to tyrosine, an amino acid. That makes T4, the storage form. T4 travels to tissues, where deiodinase enzymes trim one iodine off to make T3, the active form, or a different one to make inactive reverse T3. T3 then switches on the genes that run mitochondria and heat production. Most problems people feel come from too little raw material, poor conversion, or the body deliberately turning T3 down because it thinks food is scarce.

Get enough iodine

Iodine deficiency is the world's most common cause of thyroid disease and of preventable brain damage in children. Iodized salt fixed it in the US in the 1920s. The modern twist: people who cook at home and switch to sea salt, kosher salt or pink salt, or who cut dairy, can slide back toward low iodine, because those salts carry almost none. US iodine intake roughly halved between the early 1970s and the early 1990s. Pregnant women need more, and a shortfall then affects the baby's brain.

Salt the way you like, and pay attention to the source. Iodized table salt, seafood, dairy and eggs are the reliable sources. Raw cruciferous vegetables and soy only matter much when iodine is already low.

How: 150 mcg a day (220 in pregnancy). About ½ tsp of iodized salt gives roughly 150 mcg. Other sources: 3 oz cod (about 150 mcg), a cup of milk or yogurt (about 50–75 mcg), 1 egg (about 25 mcg), a sheet of nori (about 15–40 mcg). The upper limit is 1,100 mcg.

Eat enough, including some carbs

The body reads a calorie shortfall as famine and turns metabolism down to save fuel. T3 falls within a day or two of fasting, and reverse T3 rises. Long-term calorie restriction, very low-carb diets and hard training on little food all do the same. That's adaptive, not a disease, and TSH often stays normal. But it explains cold hands, low energy and stalled fat loss in chronic dieters. Insulin and carbohydrate help keep T4-to-T3 conversion going.

How: eat to maintain most of the time, with protein at each meal and enough carbohydrate around training. If you're cold all the time on a diet, take diet breaks at maintenance.

Get enough selenium

Gram for gram, the thyroid holds more selenium than any other organ. The deiodinases that make T3 are selenium-based enzymes, and selenium-based glutathione peroxidase protects the gland from the hydrogen peroxide it uses to make hormone. In Hashimoto's, a 2024 meta-analysis of 35 trials found selenium lowered thyroid antibodies, and lowered TSH in people not yet on medication. More isn't better. Selenium has a U-shaped curve, and high intakes raise diabetes risk.

How: about 55 mcg a day from sardines, tuna, eggs, beef and liver. With Hashimoto's, 100–200 mcg a day is the studied range. Don't go above 400.

Fix low iron

Thyroid peroxidase, the enzyme that attaches iodine to tyrosine, is iron-based. Iron deficiency slows hormone production, and in iodine-deficient children, iodine worked better once their iron was fixed. Low iron and low thyroid also cause the same symptoms: tired, cold, hair shedding. Women who menstruate are most often low. Check ferritin, not just haemoglobin, because stores run out before anaemia shows.

How: red meat, liver and shellfish give the best-absorbed iron. If you supplement, one dose every other day absorbs better than daily. Take it with vitamin C and away from coffee, tea, calcium and thyroid medication.

Bring stress down

Cortisol talks to the thyroid. High cortisol, whether from chronic stress, illness, short sleep or steroid drugs, blunts TSH release and pushes T4 toward reverse T3. Very sick hospital patients show low T3 with normal TSH for the same reason. Fixing sleep, eating enough and salting food lowers the background stress signal that keeps thyroid output down.

How: follow the Stress & Cortisol and Sleep guides: morning light, a fixed wake time, enough food and salt.

Train your cold tolerance

Cold tolerance is trainable. Brown fat burns fuel straight into heat, and cold switches on a deiodinase inside it that makes T3 locally, right where it's needed. In Dutch studies, 10 days of living at about 59–61 °F (15–16 °C) for six hours a day raised brown fat activity, and people shivered less and felt more comfortable. Regular cold water does the same faster. If you're cold all the time no matter what, though, think thyroid, iron or under-eating before you blame low brown fat.

How: start mild. A morning walk in light clothes, a cooler house, a 30–60 second cold finish to your shower. Build up over weeks. A plunge is optional, and early in the day is better.

Cover vitamin A and zinc

Inside the cell, T3 works by binding a receptor that pairs with a vitamin A receptor and grips DNA with zinc fingers. Low vitamin A raises TSH, and in a trial in children short on iodine and vitamin A, adding vitamin A lowered TSH and goitre size. Zinc deficiency lowers T3. Both are easy to cover with animal foods.

How: liver once a week, plus eggs and butter, for vitamin A. Oysters once a week, plus beef, for zinc. Keep zinc supplements under 40 mg.

Skip, or don't count on

  • Kelp pills and high-dose iodine protocols (12.5–50 mg)That's 100–300 times the daily need. Big iodine doses can shut the thyroid down (the Wolff-Chaikoff effect) or tip it into overactivity, and Hashimoto's is most vulnerable. Kelp content varies wildly.
  • "Thyroid support" glandularsLab tests of "adrenal support" pills found real thyroid hormone in all of them. Thyroid glandulars are the same idea. Stress guide
  • Avoiding broccoli and kale for your thyroidOnly matters at large raw amounts when iodine is already low. Cooked, they're fine.
  • Basal body temperature as a diagnosisA useful hint, but it moves with sleep, the menstrual cycle and the room. Get blood tests.

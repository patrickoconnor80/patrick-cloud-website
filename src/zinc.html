How to take it

  • 8–11 mg a day
  • Oysters, beef, shellfish, pumpkin seeds

Watch out

  • Upper limit 40 mg a day

Signs you're low

Mechanism

Regulates immune cell signaling and cytokine responses; supports barrier tissues (skin and mucosa).

Intake

RDA 8 mg/day (women), 11 mg/day (men); upper limit 40 mg/day (adults). Absorption is reduced by high-phytate diets (whole grains, legumes eaten without preparation methods like soaking/sprouting that reduce phytate).

↑ back to summary

Deficiency effects

Impaired immune function, poor wound healing, hair loss, taste/smell changes; in severe deficiency, growth delay and skin lesions.

Cancer risk and zinc status

Zinc deficiency creates a permissive inflammatory environment for cancer, while sufficiency appears to block tumour formation outright. Taccioli et al. (2012) fed rats a zinc-deficient diet for 21 weeks alongside the oesophageal carcinogen NMBA. Zinc-deficient animals developed oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma in 67% of cases; zinc-sufficient animals exposed to the same carcinogen developed cancer in 0%. Zinc repletion reversed the pre-malignant inflammatory gene signature at both the dysplastic and neoplastic stages — and time-course analysis confirmed the inflammation was driven by ZD rather than the carcinogen.

The mechanistic picture has two layers:

  • SOD activity. Zinc is a structural component of Cu/Zn-SOD (SOD1) in the cytosol and supports Mn-SOD (SOD2) in the mitochondrial matrix — the primary antioxidant enzymes at both sites. Deficiency reduces their activity, raises superoxide at the electron transport chain, and drives the kind of mitochondrial oxidative damage (cardiolipin peroxidation, ETC impairment) described in the cancer-as-metabolic-disease literature.
  • DNA repair and tumour suppression. Zinc finger domains are structural to p53 and several DNA-repair nucleases. ZD impairs the genomic surveillance machinery, and the deficiency-induced gene signature — 100+ upregulated inflammatory genes including S100a8, S100a9, CXC/CC chemokines, cytokines, and Cxcr-2 — was already present at the dysplastic stage before overt cancer appeared.

Animal model only. This is rat oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma with a chemical carcinogen; the human equivalent — whether dietary zinc adequacy reduces cancer incidence in a randomised trial — has not been tested. The finding is mechanistically coherent and the effect size is striking, but it cannot be extrapolated directly to humans. See also Cancer & Metabolic Disease for the broader metabolic context. Source: Taccioli C, Chen H, Jiang Y, et al. Oncogene 2012;31:4550–4558.

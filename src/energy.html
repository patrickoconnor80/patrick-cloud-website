Energy & Fatigue

Health GoalTirednessIronSleep

"Always tired" is one of the most common reasons people see a doctor, and it usually has a findable cause. Most cases come down to a few: poor or broken sleep, low iron, B12 or thyroid, blood-sugar swings, not eating enough, or being out of shape. This guide is a checklist. Work down it before reaching for an energy drink or a supplement.

An energetic week

  • Fixed wake time, outside within an hour of waking
  • Protein at breakfast, not just coffee
  • Coffee in the morning, none after noon
  • A walk every day, two or three harder sessions a week
  • Salt your food, and drink to thirst plus a glass with each meal
  • Red meat, liver or oysters once or twice a week

See a doctor if

  • Weight loss you didn't intend, night sweats or fevers
  • Getting breathless easily, chest pain or fainting
  • Low mood, loss of interest, or thoughts of self-harm
  • Crashes for days after small efforts (possible ME/CFS)
  • Tiredness lasting over a month with no obvious reason

How it fits together

Feeling energetic takes three things. First, a clock and recovery system: good sleep, and a daily rhythm of cortisol and adenosine that makes you alert by day and sleepy by night. Second, fuel and oxygen delivery: food, iron in red blood cells, thyroid hormone setting the pace, and B vitamins running the pathways that turn fuel into ATP. Third, capacity: mitochondria and muscle built by regular movement, plus enough salt and water to keep blood volume up. Fatigue is the signal that one of the three is falling short.

Fix sleep, including your breathing

Too little sleep is the most common cause of tiredness, and the effects build up: after a week or two of 6-hour nights, people perform as badly as after a night without sleep, while believing they're fine. The hidden cause is broken sleep. Obstructive sleep apnoea, where the airway collapses dozens of times an hour, affects a large share of middle-aged adults, and most aren't diagnosed. About a third of patients aren't overweight. Signs are snoring, waking unrefreshed, a dry mouth, morning headaches, and getting up to pee at night.

How: follow the Sleep guide. If you snore, or someone has seen you stop breathing, ask for a sleep study. Home tests are cheap now.

Check ferritin

Iron carries oxygen in red cells and runs the energy chain inside mitochondria. Fatigue starts when iron stores run low, before haemoglobin falls, so "you're not anaemic" doesn't rule it out. In a Swiss trial of 198 menstruating women with unexplained fatigue and ferritin under 50 µg/L, but normal haemoglobin, 12 weeks of iron cut fatigue scores by 48%, against 29% on placebo. Women with heavy periods, endurance athletes, frequent blood donors, pregnant women and people who eat little red meat are most at risk.

How: ask for ferritin and a full blood count. If ferritin is under 30, or under 50 with symptoms, eat red meat, liver and shellfish and talk to your doctor about iron. Take it every other day with vitamin C, and away from coffee, tea and calcium. Recheck after 3 months, since too much iron is harmful too.

Check thyroid and B12

Thyroid hormone sets the pace of metabolism in every cell. When it's low (hypothyroidism, most often Hashimoto's), you get heavy tiredness, feeling cold, weight gain, constipation, dry skin and slow thinking. It's especially common in women and after pregnancy. B12 is needed to make red blood cells and to keep up the insulation on nerves. Deficiency causes fatigue, breathlessness, pins and needles and brain fog, sometimes before anaemia shows. It's common in older adults, vegans, and people on metformin or acid blockers.

How: ask for TSH and free T4. Add thyroid antibodies if TSH is borderline. Ask for B12, and methylmalonic acid if B12 is in the low-normal zone. Both are cheap and treatable.

Eat enough, with protein, and steady your blood sugar

Long-term under-eating, whether from chronic dieting, skipped meals or heavy training on too little food, makes the body save energy. Thyroid output, testosterone and body temperature fall, and you feel flat. At the other end, a refined-carb breakfast or lunch spikes blood sugar and then dips below where it started a couple of hours later, which is the classic mid-afternoon crash. Thiamine (B1) and the other B vitamins are the cofactors that turn food into ATP, and heavy drinking or a diet built on refined flour runs them low.

How: eat protein at breakfast (eggs, meat, Greek yogurt), have meals rather than grazing, and eat protein and vegetables before starch. Don't run a big calorie deficit for months on end.

Move every day and build fitness

Training adds mitochondria and capillaries to muscle, so the same stairs and errands use a smaller share of your capacity and feel easier. In a meta-analysis of exercise trials, regular exercise raised people's sense of energy and lowered fatigue. In the few trials that used a convincing placebo, the effect shrank, so part of it may be expectation. Even so, deconditioning is a common, fixable cause of feeling worn out, and resting more rarely helps ordinary tiredness. The exception is ME/CFS, where exertion makes symptoms crash and pacing matters.

How: a walk every day, plus two or three sessions a week of something that makes you breathe hard or lift heavy. If you're very deconditioned, start with 10 minutes and build up.

Get salt and water

In a University of Connecticut study, young women who were about 1.4% dehydrated, after exercise or a water pill, reported lower mood, more fatigue and more headaches, and found tasks harder. Salt matters as much as water. Sodium holds water in the blood, so a low-salt diet, low-carb eating (which makes the kidneys dump sodium) or heavy sweating lowers blood volume. That can show up as lightheadedness on standing, headaches and a drained feeling, sometimes called "keto flu." It's often fixed within a day.

How: salt your home-cooked food to taste. On low-carb diets, or on hot or heavy-sweat days, add ¼–½ tsp of salt in water, or use broth. Drink to thirst, plus a glass with meals. Pale yellow urine is the target.

Get morning light, and time your caffeine

The body clock times your morning rise in cortisol and your alertness through the day, and bright morning light is what sets it. Indoor light is dim enough that the clock drifts, leaving you groggy in the morning and wired at night. Caffeine blocks adenosine, the chemical that builds up the longer you're awake. It doesn't remove it. When the caffeine wears off, the adenosine hits all at once, and caffeine within about 6 hours of bed cuts deep sleep, which makes tomorrow worse. Heavy daily use also builds tolerance, so you need it just to feel normal.

How: spend 10–30 minutes outside soon after waking. Have coffee or tea after you've been up a little while, and none after noon. If you're dependent, cut back for a week or two to reset your tolerance.

Drink less

Alcohol is a sedative going in, but as it wears off in the second half of the night it causes wake-ups, suppresses REM sleep and raises heart rate, so a few drinks costs you sleep quality even when the hours look fine. It also uses up NAD⁺ and thiamine, the cofactors that turn food into energy.

How: try two dry weeks and notice the difference. After that, keep drinks to a couple, finished 3 hours before bed.

Worth considering

  • Creatine. It helps most when you're short on sleep or under mental strain, and in vegetarians. 1 tsp (5 g) a day. Creatine
  • Vitamin D if you're low. Low vitamin D is very common in tired patients, and fatigue scores improved after correcting it, but that study had no placebo group. Test first. Vitamin D
  • Magnesium. Every ATP molecule is used bound to magnesium, and intake is often low. Glycinate helps with sleep too. Magnesium
  • Cold water. A short cold shower or plunge raises noradrenaline for an hour or two. Cold Exposure
  • Check your medicines. Antihistamines, beta-blockers, some antidepressants, sleep aids, opioids and acid blockers (through B12) can all cause fatigue.

Skip, or don't count on

  • Energy drinksBorrowed energy: caffeine, sugar and acid. You crash, sleep worse and need more. They're also hard on teeth.
  • "Adrenal fatigue" supplementsNot a real diagnosis, and Mayo Clinic testing found these pills contained thyroid hormone and steroids. Stress guide
  • B12 shots when your B12 is normalThey help if you're deficient and do little if you're not.
  • NAD⁺ IV dripsExpensive, uncomfortable, and with no trials showing they relieve fatigue.
  • Iron "just in case"Too much iron is harmful, and men and postmenopausal women rarely need it. Test ferritin first.
  • Sleeping in on weekends to catch upIt shifts your clock and makes Monday worse. A fixed wake time works better.

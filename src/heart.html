Heart Health

Health GoalArteriesBlood pressureClotting

Most heart attacks are a clot forming on a damaged plaque. Three things set your risk: what your blood particles carry, how your blood vessels behave, and how easily your blood clots. Food and training move all three.

A heart week

  • 2 × HIIT sessions (4 × 4 min hard), 2 × lifting, walk daily
  • Wild salmon or sardines 2–3 times
  • A potato, an avocado or a glass of OJ most days
  • Arugula salad or roasted beets most days
  • Salt food to taste: about 1¼–2¼ tsp of salt a day, counting what's already in food
  • 200–400 mg magnesium glycinate in the evening

Watch out

  • Don't stop a prescribed statin or blood-pressure drug on your own. Talk to your doctor first.
  • Antibacterial mouthwash kills the bacteria that turn nitrate into nitric oxide
  • No potassium pills with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone or kidney disease
  • Nattokinase thins the blood. Never take it with blood thinners or before surgery.

How it fits together

Plaque forms when apoB particles (LDL and its relatives) get stuck in the artery wall and the fats they carry oxidise there. Blood pressure and stiff vessels injure the wall and speed that up. What actually kills is a clot forming on a plaque that cracks. The statins paper builds this model in full. Here is the short version, with each habit placed where it acts.

Do hard intervals twice a week

In the CERT trial, 60 people with coronary stents did supervised 4×4 intervals twice a week for six months. Ultrasound inside their coronary arteries showed the plaque shrank, while the control group's grew. Nobody was targeting cholesterol. Training also lowers fibrinogen and resting pressure, raises VO2max and makes muscle a bigger sink for blood sugar, so it acts on all three panels above.

It was one small trial, and both groups were mostly on statins, so it shows intervals working on top of a statin, not instead of one. The intensity matters too: CERT used 85–95% of max heart rate, not easy Zone 2.

How: four 4-minute efforts at 85–95% of max heart rate (hard enough that you can't talk), with 3 minutes easy between. Bike, rower, incline treadmill or hill runs. Twice a week, plus walking and lifting on other days.

Fix insulin resistance

High insulin is upstream of most heart markers. It raises triglycerides, lowers HDL, shifts LDL toward small dense particles, makes the kidneys hold sodium and stiffens arteries. Fix insulin sensitivity and those all improve together. A cholesterol drug, by contrast, moves one number.

Insulin sensitivity is built mostly in muscle and lost mostly in belly fat. Lifting gives glucose somewhere to go. Refined starch and sugar on top of a seed-oil diet are the fastest way to lose it. Sleep counts too.

How: lift 2–3 times a week, cut refined flour and sugary drinks, and keep the starch you eat around training. Check fasting insulin and triglycerides to see where you stand.

Cook with tallow, butter or olive oil, not seed oils

An LDL particle only does damage once its fats oxidise in the artery wall, and linoleic acid, the main fat in soybean, corn, sunflower and canola oil, is the one that oxidises. The fats you eat end up in your LDL. In feeding studies, swapping linoleic acid for oleic acid made LDL measurably harder to oxidise.

Lowering the fuel is better than adding antioxidants. The vitamin C, E and beta-carotene trials all failed, and beta-carotene did harm. The weak link is that no trial has cut linoleic acid and counted heart attacks. The best old test of the opposite advice, the Minnesota Coronary Experiment, swapped saturated fat for corn oil. Cholesterol fell and deaths did not.

How: cook with tallow, butter, ghee or olive oil. Skip fried restaurant food and anything made with soybean, corn, sunflower, safflower or canola oil. Chicken and pork fed on grain carry more linoleic acid than beef or lamb.

Eat potassium-rich food every day

Potassium helps the kidneys clear sodium and relaxes the smooth muscle in artery walls. Across 22 trials, raising it lowered pressure in people with high blood pressure, and not in people whose pressure was already normal. Your sodium-to-potassium ratio predicts heart outcomes better than sodium alone. Most people eat well under the 2,600–3,400 mg target.

How: from food. A baked potato has about 900 mg, an avocado about 700 mg, a cup of orange juice about 450 mg, and a cup of cooked spinach about 840 mg. Potassium pills are kept small for a reason. High doses can stop the heart.

Eat arugula and beets

Nitrate from arugula and beets is absorbed, concentrated in saliva, turned into nitrite by bacteria on your tongue, and turned into nitric oxide in the body. This route skips the eNOS enzyme, which stops working well in metabolic disease, so it still delivers nitric oxide when the vessel lining can't make enough itself. The trials are consistent: lower blood pressure and better blood flow, with bigger effects in people who start high.

The bacteria are the catch. Antibacterial mouthwash wipes them out and the effect goes with them.

How: a big handful of arugula, 2 medium roasted beets, 1 cup of beet juice or a 70 mL beet shot. Take it 2–3 hours before you want the effect. Roast or steam beets, because boiling leaches out the nitrate.

Get enough magnesium

Magnesium blocks calcium from entering vessel muscle, which lets the wall relax. It also keeps the heart's rhythm steady and helps insulin work. Across 34 trials the effect on pressure was small but steady, and it was larger in people who were insulin resistant or low to begin with. That's the same pattern as everything else on this page: it helps most where the terrain is worst.

How: 300–420 mg a day from food plus supplements. Glycinate is well absorbed and easy on the gut. Skip oxide. Food sources are pumpkin seeds, spinach, avocado and dark chocolate.

Eat wild oily fish 2–3 times a week

EPA and DHA lower triglycerides reliably, go into heart-cell membranes, where they steady the rhythm, and calm inflammation. Eating fish tracks with fewer heart deaths in cohort studies. Capsule trials are mixed: high-dose purified EPA helped, and most fish-oil trials found little. Whole fish also brings vitamin D, selenium and protein, which may be why it does better than capsules.

Wild is worth paying for. Farmed fish raised on grain and soy feed carry more omega-6 and less omega-3. Nuts are no substitute. Their omega-3 is ALA, which the body converts poorly, and most come loaded with linoleic acid.

How: wild salmon (about 8 oz) or a can or two of sardines, 2–3 times a week.

Get midday sun on your skin

Skin stores nitric oxide compounds, and UVA light releases them into the blood. That relaxes vessels within minutes, and vitamin D has nothing to do with it. A Swedish cohort of 29,518 women found that sun-seekers had fewer heart deaths than sun-avoiders. That study can't separate sunlight from an outdoor lifestyle, but the nitric oxide release itself has been measured in humans.

How: arms and legs out for 10–30 minutes around midday, short of burning. Build up slowly in spring.

Don't cut salt if you cook at home

Most sodium comes from processed and restaurant food, so people who cook from scratch usually eat less than they think. Cutting sodium hard raises renin, aldosterone and adrenaline, and large cohorts find a J-curve: heart risk climbs at low intakes as well as very high ones. The real lever is the sodium-to-potassium ratio, and you fix that by eating more potassium, not by eating less salt.

It's contested for a reason. Salt-sensitive people, often older or Black or with kidney disease, do see their pressure rise with salt, and the low-intake cohorts include sick people who eat little. The claim that sodium has no effect on pressure at all overshoots too.

How: about 3–5 g of sodium a day. That's 1¼–2¼ tsp of salt counting what's already in food, and more if you sweat a lot.

Worth considering

  • Vitamin K2. Activates matrix Gla protein, which keeps calcium out of the artery wall. The target is arterial stiffness, not cholesterol. 90–200 mcg of MK-7 a day, or natto, eggs, dairy and liver. Vitamin K2
  • Nattokinase. Breaks down fibrin and lowers fibrinogen, which works on the clot panel. The plaque results split on dose: 6,000–10,800 FU looked good, and the cleanest trial, at 2,000 FU for three years, found nothing. It thins the blood, so never take it with blood thinners. Nattokinase · Statins paper VII.6
  • Black tea. Better vessel function in trials and fewer heart deaths in cohorts. An easy daily habit. Assam tea
  • Breathe through your nose. The sinuses make nitric oxide and nose breathing carries it into the lungs. It works locally, not as a blood-pressure fix, but it's free. Statins paper VII.5

Skip, or don't count on

  • Antioxidant pills (vitamin C, E, beta-carotene)Tested in tens of thousands of people. No heart benefit, vitamin E raised heart failure on long follow-up, and beta-carotene raised deaths in smokers. Cut the linoleic acid that oxidises instead. Why
  • "Heart-healthy" seed-oil spreads and margarineThey lower LDL-C on paper and load your LDL with the fat that oxidises. Linoleic Acid paper
  • Nuts as heart foodALA converts poorly to EPA and DHA, and most nuts are high in linoleic acid. Eat fish instead.
  • Pomegranate juice as a long-term blood-pressure fixIt works on a real target (ACE), but the effect seems to fade after about two months and a glass has around 30 g of sugar. Use it now and then. Pomegranate
  • Low-dose nattokinase (2,000 FU)The most careful trial ran three years at this dose and found nothing.

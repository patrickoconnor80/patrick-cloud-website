Energy Metabolism

How glucose, fat, and protein all become ATP — glycolysis, β-oxidation, the Krebs cycle, and amino acid entry — and the B vitamins whose coenzyme forms make every step run.

Draft v1 Scope mechanism primer, not a supplement protocol Standard textbook biochemistry throughout
Abstract

Three fuels — glucose, fat, and, when needed, protein — are broken down by three different routes that all converge on the same destination: acetyl‑CoA and the Krebs cycle. Glycolysis strips glucose to pyruvate in the cytosol; β-oxidation spirals fatty acids down two carbons at a time in the mitochondrial matrix; transamination strips the nitrogen off amino acids and hands their carbon skeletons into one of six points around the same cycle. What all three routes share is a small, fixed set of B‑vitamin‑derived coenzymes doing the actual chemistry: TPP (B1) and CoA (B5) gate carbon into and through the cycle, FAD (B2) and NAD+ (B3) carry the stripped electrons back out to the electron transport chain, and PLP (B6) runs every transamination that gets protein into the system at all.

This paper is a primer, not an argument: nothing here is contested biochemistry. It exists to make the mechanism visible — specifically, which enzyme needs which vitamin, and why a deficiency in one B vitamin stalls an identifiable step rather than causing a vague drop in “energy.”

A note on sourcing

The core pathway facts here are standard biochemistry — the kind found in Lehninger or Harper's Illustrated Biochemistry — and are not in dispute. The framing choice, to organize the pathways around which B vitamin does which specific job rather than treating “cofactors” as a footnote, follows the teaching style of Chris Masterjohn, whose nutrient-biochemistry writing and courses cover this material in more depth than a single page can.

The path in brief

Three fuels, five parts

  1. Part IGlycolysis splits glucose into two pyruvate molecules in the cytosol — the only vitamin cofactor used directly is niacin (NAD+), at a single step.
  2. Part IIβ-oxidation spirals a fatty acid down two carbons per turn in the mitochondrial matrix, needing riboflavin, niacin, and pantothenic acid every single turn.
  3. Part IIIBoth routes hand off to the Krebs cycle as acetyl-CoA — whose two decarboxylation steps are the densest cofactor dependencies in the whole system.
  4. Part IVProtein feeds in too: transamination, a PLP (B6)-dependent reaction, strips the nitrogen off any of twenty amino acids and hands the carbon skeleton into one of six points on the pathways above.
  5. Part VEight B vitamins, mapped to the specific coenzyme and the specific step each one runs — with links out to the full nutrient-level page for each.

Not eight vitamins doing something vaguely metabolic — eight specific coenzymes, each gating a specific, nameable reaction.

Part I

Glycolysis

Ten enzymatic steps, run in the cytosol without oxygen, split into two phases. The first five steps spend 2 ATP to prime glucose for cleavage; the last five, run twice over — once per 3-carbon half — repay that loan and turn a profit. Only one step in the whole sequence is a redox reaction, and it's where niacin does its work.

I.1Investment, then payoff, twice over

Hexokinase and phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) each spend one ATP to add a phosphate group, priming the six-carbon sugar to be split in half by aldolase. From there, every downstream step runs twice — once for each 3-carbon fragment. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) reduces NAD+ to NADH while oxidizing the triose; phosphoglycerate kinase and pyruvate kinase each regenerate one ATP directly from the reaction, a mechanism called substrate-level phosphorylation because it doesn't route through the electron transport chain at all.

Net per glucose: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, and 2 pyruvate. The investment phase (hexokinase, PFK-1) spends 2 ATP; the payoff phase runs twice — once per triose — repaying that loan and netting 2 ATP plus 2 NADH.

I.2The bridge to the cycle: pyruvate dehydrogenase

Five cofactors, one enzyme

Before pyruvate can enter the Krebs cycle it has to be decarboxylated to acetyl-CoA by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex — and that single enzyme needs five cofactors running at once: thiamine (TPP, B1), lipoic acid, coenzyme A (B5), FAD (B2), and NAD+ (B3). It's the densest vitamin dependency in this entire paper, and it repeats almost exactly at the α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase step inside the Krebs cycle itself (III.2) — which is why thiamine status alone can throttle carbon flow into the cycle from two separate directions.

Part II

β-Oxidation

Fat is dismantled in the mitochondrial matrix by a four-step loop that repeats, not a line that runs once. A carnitine shuttle first carries the activated fatty acyl-CoA across the inner mitochondrial membrane; from there, a C16 chain like palmitate spins through the loop seven times, releasing eight acetyl-CoA units and reducing seven FAD and seven NAD+ along the way. Every turn needs the same two vitamin-derived electron acceptors and the same pantothenic-acid-built coenzyme to cleave the final bond.

II.1Four steps, repeated until nothing is left but acetyl-CoA

Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase reduces FAD to FADH2 while introducing a double bond; enoyl-CoA hydratase adds water across it; 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase reduces NAD+ to NADH; and thiolase uses a free coenzyme A to cleave off the terminal two carbons as acetyl-CoA, leaving a chain two carbons shorter to re-enter at the top.

Per turn: 1 FADH2, 1 NADH, 1 acetyl-CoA. A C16 chain (palmitate) spins through seven turns, releasing eight acetyl-CoA for the Krebs cycle below.

Part III

The Krebs Cycle

Every acetyl-CoA — from glucose or from fat — enters the same eight-step wheel in the mitochondrial matrix. Oxaloacetate is regenerated at the end of each turn, so the cycle never runs out of a place to put the next acetyl group. Four of the eight steps are redox reactions loading electrons onto NAD+ or FAD; a fifth makes GTP directly by substrate-level phosphorylation.

III.1Eight steps, one wheel

Per turn: 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP, 2 CO2. The two decarboxylation steps and the succinate dehydrogenase step double as a direct entry point into the electron transport chain.

III.2Two steps worth noticing

Where the cycle repeats itself — and where it plugs straight into the ETC

α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase is structurally almost identical to pyruvate dehydrogenase (I.2) and needs the same five cofactors — B1, lipoic acid, B2, B3, B5 — a second checkpoint where thiamine status alone can throttle the cycle. Succinate dehydrogenase is the only Krebs cycle enzyme embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane: it's also Complex II of the electron transport chain, so this one step belongs to both pathways at once.

Part IV

Protein & Amino Acid Metabolism

Glucose and fat aren't the only fuels these pathways run on. Amino acids — from dietary protein, or from muscle during fasting, very-low-carbohydrate intake, or intake beyond what's needed for synthesis — can be stripped of their nitrogen and fed into the same central pathways as carbon skeletons. It's normally a minor contributor to ATP production, but the entry mechanism is one every amino acid shares.

IV.1Transamination strips the nitrogen — PLP does the stripping

Before an amino acid's carbon skeleton can enter glycolysis or the Krebs cycle, its amino group has to come off. That's done by a family of enzymes called aminotransferases (or transaminases — ALT and AST are the two measured on a standard liver panel), which transfer the amino group from the amino acid onto α-ketoglutarate, producing glutamate and the corresponding keto acid: alanine becomes pyruvate, aspartate becomes oxaloacetate, and so on for each of the twenty.

Every transaminase in the body runs on one cofactor

Pyridoxal-5′-phosphate (PLP), the active form of vitamin B6, is the obligate cofactor for every transamination reaction. It forms a Schiff base with the incoming amino group and physically carries it across to the accepting keto acid — the same chemistry, repeated for all twenty amino acids. Glutamate's amino group is then released as free ammonia by glutamate dehydrogenase (an NAD+/NADP+-dependent oxidative deamination), regenerating the α-ketoglutarate that started the cycle. The ammonia itself is converted to urea in the liver's urea cycle and excreted by the kidneys — an ATP-costly disposal route, and a separate system from the ATP-generating pathways this paper otherwise covers.

IV.2Six doors, twenty amino acids

Once the nitrogen is off, the carbon skeleton lands at one of six points on the pathways already drawn above: pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, or one of four Krebs cycle intermediates. Which door an amino acid uses determines what its carbon can become. Skeletons that land at pyruvate or a Krebs cycle intermediate can be run in reverse, through gluconeogenesis, back into glucose — these are called glucogenic. Leucine and lysine are the exception: they land only at acetyl-CoA, which the Krebs cycle can burn for ATP but which cell chemistry cannot run back into glucose — making them purely ketogenic. A handful of amino acids split their carbons across two doors and count as both.

Every arrow above is PLP-dependent (B6). Alanine, serine, glycine, and cysteine feed pyruvate; glutamate, glutamine, histidine, proline, and arginine feed α-ketoglutarate; aspartate and asparagine feed oxaloacetate; isoleucine, methionine, valine, and threonine feed succinyl-CoA; phenylalanine and tyrosine feed fumarate. Leucine and lysine are the only two amino acids that are purely ketogenic — every other amino acid can, at least partly, run back to glucose.

Part V

The B-Vitamin Cofactors

Each B vitamin has to be converted into an active coenzyme form before it can do anything. A deficiency doesn't cause a generic drop in “energy” — it stalls a specific, nameable step in one of the pathways above. Six of the eight get direct or indirect use in this paper; the individual nutrient pages carry deficiency signs, intake targets, and food sources for each.

VitaminCoenzymeActs inRole
B1 · Thiamine TPP I, III Cofactor for pyruvate dehydrogenase (I.2) and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (III.2) — the two decarboxylation steps that hand carbon into and around the Krebs cycle.
B2 · Riboflavin FAD / FMN II, III FAD is the electron acceptor for acyl-CoA dehydrogenase in every turn of β-oxidation and for succinate dehydrogenase in the Krebs cycle, which feeds those electrons directly into Complex II of the electron transport chain.
B3 · Niacin NAD+ / NADP+ I, II, III The most-used cofactor in this paper: GAPDH in glycolysis, 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase in β-oxidation, and three separate Krebs cycle steps all reduce NAD+ to NADH — the main electron currency feeding Complex I.
B5 · Pantothenic acid Coenzyme A I, II, III CoA is the handle every acyl group is carried by: fatty acid activation, thiolase's final cleavage in β-oxidation, and both pyruvate dehydrogenase and citrate synthase depend on a free thiol from CoA-SH.
B6 · Pyridoxine PLP IV PLP runs every transamination reaction in Part IV — swapping an amino group for a keto group and handing twenty different carbon skeletons into six points on the pathways above.
B7 · Biotin Carboxylase prosthetic group III Pyruvate carboxylase, biotin's signature reaction, converts pyruvate directly to oxaloacetate — refilling the Krebs cycle whenever intermediates are pulled out for amino acid or heme synthesis.
Reading the map

Four cofactors do almost all the direct work: TPP and CoA gate carbon entering and moving through the cycle, while FAD and NAD+ carry the electrons back out to the electron transport chain. B6 and biotin don't touch the core pathways directly — B6 keeps protein's carbon flowing in, and biotin keeps the cycle's intermediate pool topped up — which is what lets the Krebs cycle double as a biosynthetic hub, not just a combustion engine.

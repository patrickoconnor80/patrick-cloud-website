Immune System

Health GoalInfectionsInflammationRecovery

You don't want a "boosted" immune system. You want one that keeps germs out, hits hard when something gets in, and then switches off. The nutrients that run each of those steps are well known, and the biggest non-nutrient lever is sleep.

An immune week

  • 7–9 hours of sleep, same wake time
  • Sun in summer. Vitamin D in winter if you live north of about 37°N.
  • Oysters once, red meat a few times (zinc)
  • Liver once, eggs and butter (vitamin A)
  • Vitamin C foods daily: peppers, kiwi, citrus
  • Wild salmon or sardines 2–3 times, a fermented food daily

When you're coming down with something

  • Zinc acetate lozenges within the first day
  • Sleep more, train less. Hard exercise while sick can be dangerous with a fever.
  • Broth or salty soup for fluid and salt
  • Honey for a cough (not for babies under 1)
  • High fever, trouble breathing or chest pain need a doctor

How it fits together

Immunity works in layers. Skin, the gut lining and the mucus in your airways keep most germs out. Anything that gets through meets the first responders: neutrophils and macrophages that eat invaders and release antimicrobial peptides. Then, over days, T and B cells mount a targeted attack. Just as important is the last step, shutting the response down. An immune system that can't switch off gives you the chronic, low-grade inflammation behind most modern disease.

"Boosting" the response isn't the goal. Allergies and autoimmunity are an immune system that's too active.

Sleep at least 7 hours

In a Carnegie Mellon study, 164 adults had their sleep measured for a week, then had a cold virus dripped into their noses and were kept in quarantine. Those who'd slept under 6 hours a night were about four times as likely to get a cold as those sleeping over 7. Sleep is when T cells move to the lymph nodes and immune memory consolidates. People who slept normally after a vaccine made more antibodies than people kept awake.

How: 7–9 hours, with a fixed wake time. When you feel something coming on, add an hour.

Keep vitamin D up all year

When a macrophage detects bacteria, it pulls vitamin D from the blood, activates it inside the cell and uses it to make cathelicidin, an antimicrobial peptide that kills bacteria, including tuberculosis. Low blood vitamin D blunts this. Pooled data from 25 trials found vitamin D cut acute respiratory infections overall, and by about two-thirds in people who started very low. Daily or weekly dosing worked. Big occasional doses didn't. In VITAL, 2,000 IU a day for 5 years also cut new autoimmune disease by 22%.

How: sun on your skin in summer. In winter, if you live north of about 37°N (roughly San Francisco to Richmond), there isn't enough UVB to make any, so take 600–2,000 IU a day. Wild salmon and sardines help. Pair it with magnesium and K2.

Get enough zinc

The thymus trains T cells using a hormone that only works with zinc bound to it. Low zinc means a shrinking thymus and fewer new T cells. Zinc is also needed to switch on IL-2, the signal that tells T cells to multiply, and to switch on A20, the brake that stops inflammation afterwards. Young men with severe zinc deficiency in 1960s Iran and Egypt mostly died of infections by 25. Older adults often run low.

For colds, zinc lozenges are worth trying at the first sign. Trials disagree, but the positive ones used zinc acetate lozenges, at least 75 mg a day in total, started within 24 hours. Zinc in the throat seems to block the virus locally.

How: 8–11 mg a day from oysters (six cover a week), beef and shellfish. For a cold, zinc acetate lozenges every 2–3 hours while awake, for no more than a week. Long-term supplements stay under 40 mg, or copper drops.

Eat real vitamin A

Vitamin A keeps the linings of the airways and gut moist, mucus-making and quick to renew. Without it they dry out and turn into skin-like tissue that germs cross easily. Gut immune cells also use it to send T and B cells back to the gut wall and to make IgA. The evidence is unusually strong: in a Cochrane review of over a million children, vitamin A cut deaths from all causes by 12%. Well-fed adults rarely need supplements, but plant carotenes convert poorly in many people, so animal sources matter.

How: 700–900 mcg a day. Liver once a week covers most of it, and eggs, butter and cheese fill in.

Take vitamin C regularly

Neutrophils, the first white cells to arrive, concentrate vitamin C to many times blood levels, and use it to move, kill and then clear themselves away. A Cochrane review found that taking vitamin C every day doesn't stop ordinary people catching colds. It does shorten colds by about 8% in adults and 14% in children. In people under heavy physical stress, like marathon runners and soldiers in the cold, it roughly halved the number of colds. Starting it only after you're sick did little.

How: vitamin C foods daily: red peppers, kiwi, oranges, black currants. If you train hard or it's a stressful season, add 500–1,000 mg a day, from acerola or ascorbic acid.

Move most days

Each moderate workout sends immune cells out of storage and into the tissues to patrol, and in cohort studies people who exercise most days get fewer and milder respiratory infections. Exercise also lowers chronic inflammation over time. Very long, hard efforts, like marathon racing, are followed by a short dip, and the vitamin C results come from exactly those people.

How: walk daily, train 3–4 times a week. Back off when you're sick: an easy walk is fine for a head cold, but rest with a fever or body aches.

Feed the gut

Most of the body's immune cells sit in the gut wall, and they're trained by the bacteria next to them. Butyrate from fiber fermentation turns on regulatory T cells, the ones that calm things down. In the Stanford fermented-food trial, ten weeks of yogurt, kefir, kimchi and sauerkraut lowered 19 inflammatory proteins in the blood. A leaky gut lining does the opposite: bacterial products reach the blood and keep the immune system on low alert.

How: follow the Gut guide: a fermented food daily, fiber you tolerate, no emulsifiers, and go easy on NSAIDs and alcohol.

Eat wild oily fish

Ending inflammation is an active process. Immune cells make resolvins and protectins from EPA and DHA to stop the response and clear the debris. Arachidonic acid, from the omega-6 side, mostly makes the molecules that start inflammation. A diet heavy in seed oils and light on fish tips the balance toward starting fires and away from putting them out. Wild fish also brings vitamin D and selenium.

How: wild salmon (about 8 oz) or a can or two of sardines, 2–3 times a week. Cut seed oils.

Worth considering

  • Chicken soup and bone broth when sick. Replaces salt and fluid, and in the lab homemade chicken soup slowed the neutrophils that clog airways. Bone Broth · Chicken Soup
  • Honey for a cough. Did better than no treatment and about as well as cough syrups in children's trials. Not for babies under 1.
  • Selenium. Needed for antibodies and for clearing viruses. Low selenium let flu mutate into a nastier strain in mice. Fish and eggs cover it. Selenium
  • Glycine. Calms overactive macrophages and is needed for glutathione. Glycine

Skip, or don't count on

  • "Immune booster" blendsA more active immune system isn't the goal. Allergies and autoimmune disease are immunity that's too strong. Fill shortfalls instead.
  • EchinaceaTrials are mixed and mostly small. The well-run ones found little effect on how long colds last.
  • Colloidal silverNo benefit, and it can permanently turn your skin blue-grey (argyria).
  • Vitamin C only once you're sickRegular intake is what shortened colds. Starting late did little.
  • Big monthly vitamin D dosesDaily or weekly dosing protected against infection. Big occasional doses didn't.

Mechanism

Works through one-carbon carriers (THF/5-MTHF), supporting thymidylate and purine synthesis, and feeding the B12-dependent conversion of homocysteine back to methionine. This is the pathway the Metabolic Health hub's L-5-MTHF supplement entry bypasses directly for people with a documented folate gap or MTHFR variant.

↑ back to summary

Intake

RDA roughly 400 mcg DFE/day for adults; ~600 mcg DFE/day in pregnancy. Upper limit for synthetic folic acid is 1,000 mcg/day (adults).

↑ back to summary

Deficiency effects

Megaloblastic anemia, fatigue, glossitis; elevated homocysteine. In pregnancy, increased risk of neural tube defects. Neurologic symptoms point toward B12 deficiency rather than folate alone — worth distinguishing since they're often discussed together.

↑ back to summary

Excess effects

Excess folic acid may mask an underlying B12 deficiency, delaying diagnosis. At doses above roughly 400 mcg, hepatic DHFR (the enzyme that reduces synthetic folic acid to usable 5-MTHF) becomes saturated, and unmetabolized folic acid (UMFA) circulates in blood — a form not produced by dietary folate and whose downstream effects are still being characterized.

The folate paradox: deficiency initiates, excess promotes

Folate's relationship with cancer is stage-dependent, which is why the same nutrient appears to both protect against and accelerate cancer depending on context.

Deficiency side. Folate is required for thymidylate synthesis and for maintaining DNA methylation patterns. Chronic deficiency produces uracil misincorporation into DNA, strand breaks, and hypomethylation of gene promoters — all of which increase the probability of initiating a malignant mutation. This is the rationale for mandatory fortification in the first place.

Excess side. Once a pre-cancerous lesion exists, the picture inverts. Rapidly proliferating cells — including adenoma cells — have high folate demand to sustain DNA synthesis. Supplying extra synthetic folic acid can accelerate that replication. A randomised trial (Cole et al., JAMA 2007, n=1,021) found that 1 mg/day of folic acid in patients who had already had an adenoma removed increased advanced adenoma recurrence at three years compared to placebo. Synthetic folic acid, in people who already have polyps, appears to promote progression rather than prevention.

The fortification signal. Mandatory folic acid fortification began in the US in 1996 and Canada in 1997. Both countries saw an uptick in colorectal cancer incidence shortly after. Mason et al. (Cancer Epidemiol Biomarkers Prev 2007) noted this temporal association and hypothesised that fortification may have been "wholly or partly responsible" by feeding subclinical adenomas present in an aging population. The correlation does not establish causation — other factors changed simultaneously — but it aligns with the Cole trial mechanism.

Form matters. The concern is specific to synthetic folic acid (pteroylmonoglutamic acid) at supplemental doses, not to dietary folate (naturally occurring as polyglutamate forms reduced to 5-MTHF before absorption). Food-based folate does not produce UMFA and does not carry the same adenoma-promotion signal in the data. The practical takeaway for supplementation: get folate from food where possible; if supplementing, prefer L-5-MTHF over folic acid, particularly if you have an MTHFR variant that further limits folic acid conversion.

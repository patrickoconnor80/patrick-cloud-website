Creatine (Monohydrate)

CompoundMuscleBrainAging

The fastest backup battery your muscles and brain have. It's the best-supported supplement on this site.

At a glance

Worth taking daily
  • More strength and power from training It recharges ATP faster, so you get more out of each set. why ↓
  • Keeps muscle as you age Older adults who lift gain about 1.4 kg more lean mass with it. why ↓
  • Sharper memory, especially when tired The brain uses creatine as an energy buffer too. The benefit is biggest in vegetarians, older people and the sleep-deprived. why ↓
  • Faster response to antidepressants Adding 5 g to an SSRI sped up recovery in women with depression. why ↓
  • Helps immune cells fight tumors (in mice) Immune cells use creatine for energy too. But other mouse studies found it helped cancer spread. why ↓
  • Firmer skin (as a cream) A creatine face cream reduced sagging and wrinkles in one study run by the company that sells it. why ↓
Strong human evidence Some human evidence Early (animal / cell)

How to take it

  • 1 level tsp (3–5 g) of creatine monohydrate a day, any time
  • Loading (4 tsp / 20 g a day, split up, for 5–7 days) is optional. It just fills up faster
  • Plain monohydrate. Other forms are no better
  • Food: ~1 g per pound of beef or fish, much of it lost in cooking

The Research

Mechanisms, studies and evidence grades for each claim above.

Mechanism

Phosphocreatine donates a phosphate group to ADP via creatine kinase, regenerating ATP faster than glycolysis or oxidative phosphorylation can. That's why it matters most for short, high-intensity efforts (roughly under 3 minutes) rather than endurance output. It also shuttles high-energy phosphate from mitochondria to where ATP is used in the cell. The body makes about 1 g/day from glycine, arginine and methionine (via SAMe, which makes creatine synthesis a large consumer of methyl groups; see Methionine). Meat and fish supply roughly another 1 g/day. Muscle holds ~95% of the body's creatine, but the brain, heart, retina and testes also rely on it. Supplementing raises muscle stores by roughly 20–40%, most in people starting low.

More strength and power from training

This is one of the most replicated findings in sports nutrition. A meta-analysis of 53 RCTs (1,138 participants) found creatine improved upper-body strength (bench and chest press) regardless of age, sex, training status or dosing protocol.1 Companion analyses by the same group show the same for lower-body strength. The effect is moderate per session, but it adds up over months because each workout is a bit more productive. (Strong)

Keeps muscle as you age

A meta-analysis of 22 RCTs in 721 older adults (mean age 57–70) doing resistance training found creatine added 1.37 kg of lean mass over placebo, along with greater chest-press and leg-press strength.2 Losing muscle is one of the strongest predictors of frailty and death in old age. Creatine plus lifting is one of the few interventions that reliably pushes the other way. (Strong — for creatine with training; creatine alone does little)

Sharper memory, especially when tired

The brain uses about 20% of the body's energy and runs its own creatine–phosphocreatine buffer. Evidence that supplementing helps:

  • Vegetarians: 5 g/day for 6 weeks in 45 young vegetarian adults significantly improved working memory (backward digit span) and reasoning (Raven's matrices) in a double-blind crossover.3
  • Review: a systematic review of 6 RCTs (281 people) found short-term memory and reasoning improved, but other cognitive domains were mixed. Young, well-fed meat-eaters showed little change, and the benefit concentrated in vegetarians, older adults and people under stress.4
  • Sleep deprivation: a single high dose (0.35 g/kg, ~25 g for a 70 kg adult) taken during a 21-hour sleep deprivation changed brain phosphocreatine and pH on MR spectroscopy and improved cognitive performance and processing speed.5 This is a small study, and the dose is several times the daily norm.

The pattern is clear: creatine helps the brain most when its energy supply is under strain. (Moderate — small RCTs, consistent in the groups that are low or stressed)

Faster response to antidepressants

In 52 women with major depression starting escitalopram, adding 5 g/day creatine produced significantly greater improvement on the Hamilton Depression scale than placebo, starting as early as week 2 and holding through week 8.6 Brain-imaging follow-up by the same group linked this to changes in frontal energy metabolism. The trial involved women only, and it hasn't been replicated at scale. (Moderate — one well-designed RCT)

How to take it

  • Dose: 3–5 g/day (a level teaspoon of monohydrate is about 5 g), every day. Timing doesn't matter much. Muscle saturates in 3–4 weeks at this dose.
  • Loading: 20 g/day split into four doses for 5–7 days saturates muscle in a week. It's optional, and more likely to cause stomach upset.
  • Form: creatine monohydrate. Micronized dissolves better. HCl, ethyl ester, buffered and "liquid" creatine are no better, and some are worse.
  • Brain dosing: the brain takes up creatine more slowly than muscle, so the cognitive trials that worked used weeks of 5 g/day or more. Some researchers suggest ~10 g/day for brain goals, though this hasn't been directly tested against 5 g.
  • Food: beef, pork, herring, salmon and tuna have about 3–5 g per kg raw. Cooking destroys part of it, so diet alone rarely reaches supplement doses.
  • Who benefits most: people who eat little meat or fish, older adults, anyone lifting, and anyone short on sleep.

Safety

Kidneys: the International Society of Sports Nutrition's review of the full literature found no harm to kidneys or liver in healthy people at recommended doses, including studies lasting up to 5 years.7 But creatine is broken down into creatinine, so it raises serum creatinine and lowers the calculated eGFR on a standard blood test. That can look like kidney disease on paper. Tell your doctor you take it, or ask for a cystatin-C eGFR, which creatine doesn't affect. People with existing kidney disease should talk to their doctor first.

Hair: in one study of 20 college rugby players, 3 weeks of creatine raised dihydrotestosterone (DHT) by 56% during loading and kept it 40% above baseline during maintenance. Testosterone didn't change.8 No study has shown actual hair loss, and this single result hasn't been replicated, but men prone to male-pattern baldness may reasonably want to watch for it. Water weight: 1–2 kg is typical in the first weeks. The water is inside muscle cells, not under the skin. Stomach: GI upset mainly occurs with large single doses.

Cancer: the evidence cuts both ways

Creatine's energy buffer isn't just for muscle and brain. Any cell under heavy, fluctuating energy demand can use it, and that includes both the immune cells that attack a tumor and the tumor cells themselves. The mouse literature has found both effects:

Helps anti-tumor immunity

  • T cells (2019): Lili Yang's lab at UCLA found tumor-infiltrating CD8 T cells sharply increase the creatine transporter (CrT, Slc6a8). Knocking it out crippled anti-tumor T-cell immunity. Supplementing creatine suppressed tumor growth in several mouse tumor models, and the effect needed an intact immune system.10
  • Dendritic cells (2026): the same lab found intratumoral dendritic cells, which "teach" T cells what to attack, also raise CrT. Dendritic cells without it activated poorly and primed CD8 T cells poorly. Creatine kept their ATP up, boosted their activation, and slowed B16-OVA melanoma growth. Human monocyte-derived dendritic cells responded the same way in a dish.9 Note: the mice got creatine by daily injection (10.5 mg per mouse), the tumor experiment used 6 mice per group, and standard mouse chow has no creatine, so control mice started from a low baseline. The senior author has patents on this use filed by UCLA.

Helps cancer spread

  • Metastasis (2021): in orthotopic mouse models, dietary creatine or the body's own creatine synthesis (via the enzyme GATM, raised in liver metastases) promoted colorectal and breast cancer metastasis and shortened survival. It did this by switching on the Snail/Slug program that lets cancer cells migrate.11
  • Liver metastasis (2015): colon cancer cells that reach the oxygen-poor liver release creatine kinase B, which makes phosphocreatine outside the cell. The cancer cells then import it through the same SLC6A8 transporter to fuel their survival. Blocking that transporter suppressed metastasis in mice.12

Humans

The only human data is one cross-sectional NHANES analysis. In 25,879 US adults, each standard-deviation increase in dietary creatine intake was associated with 5% lower odds of having ever had cancer (OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.91–0.99).13 That's a weak association, and it can't show cause and effect. Dietary creatine is really a marker of meat and fish intake, and it measures cancer at the same moment it measures diet, so people already diagnosed may have changed how they eat. (Early — no human trial has tested creatine for cancer in either direction)

Bottom line: creatine may help the immune system attack a primary tumor and may also help cancer cells survive once they spread. These mouse findings don't say which effect wins in people. For healthy people this is no reason to start or stop creatine, and the decades of human safety data show no cancer signal. For someone with active cancer, especially colorectal or breast cancer, or on immunotherapy, it's a question for the oncologist. The same "energy and antioxidant support don't pick sides" logic is on the NAC page, and it fits the Cancer paper's metabolic framing.

Firmer skin (as a cream)

Skin fibroblasts use the creatine kinase system too, and energy supply declines in aging skin. In cultured human fibroblasts, creatine increased collagen gene expression and procollagen production.14,15 In the clinical part of the study, 43 men applied a face cream containing creatine, guarana extract and glycerol for 6 weeks. Jowl sagging, crow's feet, under-eye wrinkles and eye bags all improved compared with their own baseline.14 A companion study found a folic acid plus creatine cream improved firmness in mature skin.15

The limits are significant. Both papers come out of Beiersdorf's R&D department in Hamburg, and Beiersdorf sells creatine creams under the Nivea brand. There was no placebo-cream control, so glycerol (a humectant that plumps skin on its own), guarana, the moisturizing base and the placebo effect can't be separated from creatine. And it was a cream: nothing here shows that oral creatine reaches the skin or changes it. (Early — in-vitro plus one uncontrolled, industry-run cream study; no evidence for oral creatine)

Claims on X, checked

Two posts from 28 September 2026: Dalton (@Outdoctrination, "Biomolecular Engineer", sells paid consultations) on skin and cancer risk, and Daniel Tawfik (@dantawfik, molecular biologist from UCLA, founder of gethealthspan.com) summarizing the UCLA dendritic-cell paper. Each claim was checked against the primary paper.

ClaimWhat the literature showsDoes it hold up?
"People using a creatine face product showed improvements in multiple wrinkles / sagging metrics" (Dalton) True as reported. 43 men, 6 weeks, a cream with creatine, guarana and glycerol, measured against their own baseline with no placebo cream. The study came from Beiersdorf (Nivea) R&D.14 Holds for the cream, weakly. It says nothing about the oral powder most readers take. A reply to Dalton asked whether mixing monohydrate into a cream would work; nobody has tested that either.
"Creatine was shown to stimulate collagen production in skin cells" (Dalton) Yes, in cultured fibroblasts and collagen gels.14,15 In a dish. A plausible mechanism, but not evidence of anything in a living person's skin.
"Creatine is strongly linked to a decreased risk of getting cancer" (Dalton's quoted thread) NHANES cross-sectional study: 5% lower odds per SD of dietary creatine, a confidence interval that nearly touches 1 (0.91–0.99), and no supplement data.13 Overstated. It's a weak link, not a strong one; it measures meat intake, not supplements; and it can't separate cause from reverse causation.
"Pretty much any nutrient can 'stimulate cancer cells'" (Dalton, answering a reply) Creatine has specific mouse evidence of promoting metastasis through its own pathways (GATM, SLC6A8, creatine kinase B).11,12 A deflection. The replier was pointing at a real, creatine-specific literature that neither post mentions.
Creatine "powers the immune cells that direct your body's cancer-fighting response" (Tawfik) Accurately describes the UCLA findings: CrT knockout, the ATP mechanism, the human-cell experiments, and B16 melanoma growth slowed.9 Holds in mice. A fair headline for the biology, and Tawfik lists the limits (one tumor model, young mice, transplanted tumors).
"Mice received either regular drinking water or water supplemented with creatine" (Tawfik) The paper's methods say daily intraperitoneal injection of 10.5 mg creatine.9 Wrong. That matters for translation. Injection skips the gut, so "oral creatine did this" is not what was shown.
"When the researchers depleted dendritic cells, creatine supplementation no longer suppressed tumor growth" (Tawfik) I found no dendritic-cell depletion experiment in the paper's text or methods. The dendritic-cell link rests on the knockout and on correlations in tumor tissue. Not in the paper. This is the post's strongest causal claim, and it appears to have been invented. The whole summary reads like an AI-generated writeup.
Doses "within the range commonly used by athletes (3–5 g/day)" (Tawfik) 10.5 mg/day in a ~20 g mouse is ~500 mg/kg. Scaled by body-surface area, that's roughly 40 mg/kg, or about 3 g/day for a 70 kg human. Roughly right, but for injected creatine in animals whose diet has none, which isn't the same as oral creatine on top of a meat-containing diet.
"Previous studies... showed creatine enhances CD8 T cell function" (Tawfik) Correct: Di Biase et al. 2019, from the same UCLA lab.10 Holds, though it's the same group, not independent replication. The metastasis papers go unmentioned.

Verdict: neither post changes how to use creatine. It remains the best-supported supplement on this site for muscle, aging and brain, at 3–5 g/day. The skin claim applies only to a cream. The cancer story is interesting mouse biology that points both ways, and both posts told only the flattering half.

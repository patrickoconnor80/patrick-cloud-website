About the data

  • Source: USDA FoodData Central, SR Legacy (2018 release), as per-100 g values scaled to the serving shown. Meat and fish servings are 4 oz cooked. Click a food's name to see the exact USDA entry it comes from.
  • Targets are the US RDA, or the AI where no RDA exists, for adults 19–50. Glycine's 10 g is this site's estimate from the Glycine / Collagen page, not an official figure. Each nutrient has its own page in the index.
  • Omega-3 and omega-6. EPA+DHA is the long-chain omega-3 from fish, against a 500 mg daily target (there's no official RDA). ALA is the plant omega-3, shown in grams without a target because little of it becomes EPA or DHA. LA (linoleic acid, the main omega-6) is shown as the share of the food's own calories: the Linoleic Acid paper puts the need at about 1–2% of calories, against typical intake of 8–10%.
  • Brand products use the manufacturer's nutrition label, wherever it gives a number. Labels print only a few nutrients, so the rest are estimated from the ingredient list: each ingredient's USDA values, scaled so protein and fat match the label. For example, Wild River Bites' omega-3s come from USDA carp scaled to the pack's 3 g of fat, and the collagen's glycine from gelatin. The venison jerky bites publish micronutrients but no calorie, protein or sodium panel, so those three are estimated too. Click a product's name to see the ingredient model. Guacamole is computed from a simple recipe of USDA ingredients.
  • Bone broth is partly estimated. USDA has only a thin home-made beef stock, so protein is set to the median commercial bone broth, and glycine is estimated at 22% of protein, the ratio in gelatin.
  • Gaps in USDA's data are estimated and marked ~. Glycine comes from protein, using the typical glycine share for that kind of food. Other blanks come from the closest USDA entry: farmed trout for wild trout, raw shellfish for cooked (adjusted for protein), and so on. Anything left over is a nutrient the food has little or none of, and is set to 0. None of these need to be exact to be useful, but a measured number beats a ~.
  • Absorption isn't reflected. Iron in plant foods is non-heme and much less absorbed. Plant vitamin A is beta-carotene, which converts to retinol inefficiently. Foods where this matters are flagged in their detail row.
  • Niacin is preformed niacin only. Protein-rich foods also supply tryptophan, which the body converts to niacin.

The Research

How it works, then what it does in each part of the body. Where a section says "no human studies", the case rests on physiology and animal work.

What it is

Black seed is the small black seed of Nigella sativa, a flowering plant grown from the Mediterranean to India. It's also sold as black cumin or kalonji, and it has been used as food and medicine across the Middle East and South Asia for over two thousand years. Pressing the seed gives black seed oil.

The compound that matters most is thymoquinone (TQ), which makes up a small fraction of the oil but accounts for most of its effects. The seed also carries related compounds (thymohydroquinone, nigellone, carvacrol), and the oil itself is mostly fat: about 50–60% linoleic acid and 20–25% oleic acid.

How it works

Thymoquinone does four things that show up again and again across tissues. Nearly every benefit below is one of these four at work in a different organ.

  • It turns down inflammation. TQ suppresses NF-κB, the master switch that tells cells to make inflammatory signals such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6. It also blocks the enzymes COX-2 and 5-lipoxygenase, which make prostaglandins and leukotrienes.
  • It turns up the body's own antioxidants. TQ activates NRF2, the switch that tells cells to make protective enzymes: superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase and others. That's a more durable effect than an antioxidant that just mops up free radicals directly.
  • It adjusts the immune system. TQ affects how T cells and B cells multiply and activate. In practice it seems to steady the immune system rather than simply boosting or suppressing it.
  • It pushes damaged cells to self-destruct. In injured and cancerous cells, TQ triggers apoptosis, the body's orderly way of clearing cells that are beyond repair.

Cellular energy: NQO1 and NAD⁺ recycling

Thymoquinone is a quinone, which makes it fuel for an enzyme called NQO1. NQO1 does two useful things in one step. It converts NADH back into NAD⁺, which mitochondria need to keep burning fuel. And it turns the quinone into a form that works as a recyclable antioxidant. NQO1 does this cleanly, without creating the unstable radical that other enzymes produce when they handle quinones.

TQ isn't the only compound that feeds this enzyme. CoQ10, PQQ and beta-lapachone (from Pau d'arco) do the same. NQO1 itself is switched on by NRF2, so anything that activates NRF2, such as exercise or sulforaphane from broccoli, supports the same pathway.

TQ also activates AMPK and SIRT1, the energy-sensing pathways that fasting and exercise switch on and that are tied to healthy aging. In aged rats, TQ restored SIRT1 activity and reversed age-related damage in tissue.19 No human studies have measured these pathways after taking black seed. Blood sugar, cholesterol and blood pressure

This is the most-studied use. A 2025 analysis pooled 82 trials with 5,026 people, using 200 mg to 4.6 g a day for up to 48 weeks. Black seed improved fasting blood sugar, HbA1c, insulin sensitivity, LDL, triglycerides, HDL, blood pressure, body weight and waist size.1

The effects are modest, and the individual trials are small. The mechanism fits: activating AMPK tells the liver to make less glucose and tells muscle to take up more, and less inflammation improves how cells respond to insulin. Black seed belongs alongside diet for metabolic health, not in place of it (see Metabolic Health).

Allergies, asthma and histamine

Allergy symptoms come from mast cells, which release histamine and leukotrienes when they meet an allergen. Black seed works on both. Thymoquinone and nigellone steady mast cells so they release less histamine, and TQ blocks 5-lipoxygenase, the enzyme that makes leukotrienes. Leukotrienes are what tighten the airways in asthma.

The human trials agree. Across 8 hay-fever trials, people taking black seed were about four times as likely to get relief, with fewer nasal symptoms.2 Across 4 asthma trials, asthma-control scores and lung function improved.3 In a trial of a standardized oil (5% thymoquinone), nasal and eye symptoms both eased within 15 days.4 That trial was run by the oil's manufacturer.

Inflammation and oxidative stress

Across 20 trials with 1,086 people, black seed lowered C-reactive protein and TNF-α, two standard blood markers of inflammation. It also lowered MDA, a marker of fat oxidation, and raised the body's own antioxidant enzymes, superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase.5 That matches the two core mechanisms above: less NF-κB activity and more NRF2 activity.

The same NRF2 theme runs through several other supplements on this site, including beets and Chinese skullcap.

Liver

In people with fatty liver, five trials with 358 participants found that black seed lowered the liver enzymes ALT and AST, lowered fasting blood sugar and CRP, and reduced the grade of fat in the liver on ultrasound.6

The liver is where the mechanisms overlap most. AMPK shifts the liver from making fat to burning it, and less oxidative stress means less of the damage that turns a fatty liver into an inflamed one. For liver scarring specifically, see Milk Thistle.

Stomach and gut

Black seed protects the stomach lining and reduces the inflammatory cells that gather in it. In people with chronic indigestion, a honey-based black seed oil preparation eased symptoms and lowered rates of H. pylori infection when added to standard acid-reducing treatment.7

Black seed also works against H. pylori, the bacterium behind most ulcers. In one trial, 2 g of ground seed a day with an acid blocker cleared the infection in 67% of patients, against 83% for standard triple antibiotics.8 That makes it a useful add-on, not a replacement for antibiotics.

Candida, fungal infections and SIBO

In the lab, thymoquinone kills Candida yeast, breaks up the protective films it forms, and switches off the genes it uses to invade tissue.17 It also kills a wide range of bacteria, including some antibiotic-resistant strains.

That's the basis for using black seed against candida overgrowth and SIBO (small intestinal bacterial overgrowth). The same antimicrobial action is what works against H. pylori in the stomach, where it has been confirmed in people. No human studies have tested black seed for candida, other fungal infections or SIBO.

Skin and hair

For skin, black seed works best applied directly. Across 14 trials with 732 people, black seed creams, gels and oils improved eczema, acne, psoriasis and vitiligo.9 In hand eczema, a black seed ointment worked as well as betamethasone, a prescription steroid cream, and better than a plain moisturizer.10

It works on skin for the same reasons it works elsewhere: it calms inflammation, kills the bacteria and yeasts that aggravate acne and dandruff, and helps skin cells make collagen and close wounds.

For hair, the reasoning is that a calmer, less inflamed scalp with less yeast is a better place for hair to grow. No human studies on hair growth have been published in the major medical journals.

Kidneys

In a trial of 60 people with kidney stones, half took ¼ tsp (500 mg) of black seed twice a day for 10 weeks. In that group, 44% passed their stones completely and another 52% saw them shrink. On placebo, 15% passed their stones.11

In people with kidney disease from diabetes, ½ tsp of black seed oil a day for 12 weeks lowered creatinine, urea and protein in the urine, and raised the kidneys' filtering rate.12 In animals, TQ protects kidney tissue from oxidative damage. Anyone with kidney disease should stick to these doses and tell their doctor; see Safety.

Brain

In 40 healthy older adults, ¼ tsp (500 mg) of black seed twice a day for nine weeks improved scores on tests of memory, attention and mental speed, compared with placebo.13 A companion trial found better mood and less anxiety in teenage boys.

In animals, TQ protects brain cells in several ways. It raises BDNF, the brain's main growth factor. It slows the breakdown of acetylcholine, the memory neurotransmitter, which is how some Alzheimer's drugs work. It also reduces the build-up of amyloid and helps clear it.

Immune system and autoimmunity

Black seed seems to balance the immune system, strengthening a weak response and calming an overactive one.

  • Healthy people: ½ tsp (1 g) of ground seed a day for four weeks raised total lymphocytes and helper T cells in young adults. Doubling the dose lost the effect, which is one reason not to take more than the usual amount.14
  • Hashimoto's thyroiditis: eight weeks of ground seed lowered TSH and the anti-thyroid antibodies that drive the disease, and raised the active thyroid hormone T3.15
  • Rheumatoid arthritis: two 500 mg capsules of oil a day (about ¼ tsp) for eight weeks raised IL-10, the immune system's main calming signal, and lowered markers of oxidative stress.16

Eyes

The retina burns more oxygen for its size than almost any other tissue, which makes it prone to oxidative damage. In animal models of glaucoma, diabetic eye disease and retinal injury, thymoquinone protects the retina and optic nerve by lowering oxidative stress and inflammation and by keeping nerve cells alive.18

No human studies have tested black seed for eye disease. Itchy, watery allergy eyes did improve in the hay-fever trial above.

How to take it

  • Oil: ½–1 tsp a day. The trials used ¼–½ tsp (1–2.5 g). It tastes sharp and peppery, so most people take it with honey, in a smoothie, or as capsules.
  • Ground seed: ½–1 tsp (1–2 g) a day, stirred into food or a smoothie. Grind about a week's worth at a time and store it airtight and away from light, because the oils in the seed go stale once it's cracked.
  • With fat: thymoquinone absorbs poorly on its own and needs fat to carry it. Take it with a meal.
  • On skin: rub the oil directly on the area once or twice a day. Try a small patch first.
  • How long: most trials ran 4–12 weeks before measuring a benefit.

Oil or ground seed?

Both work, and the trials above used both. Each has a trade-off.

  • Oil is concentrated and easy to take, and it's the form to use on skin. Its weakness is consistency. Thymoquinone content in commercial oils ranges from about 0.07% to 1.9%, a 27-fold spread. Choose a cold-pressed oil in dark glass that states its thymoquinone percentage on the label. The oil is also 50–60% linoleic acid, though at a teaspoon a day that's only about 2.5 g (see Linoleic Acid).
  • Ground seed avoids the potency lottery and brings the seed's fiber with it. It takes a little more effort, and it isn't suited to skin.

Safety

Don't megadose. At normal doses black seed has been taken safely in trials lasting up to 48 weeks. At high doses, animals show lethargy and weight loss. The chemistry explains why: at normal doses thymoquinone is recycled by NQO1 as an antioxidant, but in large amounts quinones generate oxidative stress instead. Cancer cells make unusually large amounts of NQO1, and some chemotherapy drugs are quinones designed to exploit that (see Cancer as Metabolic Disease).

Medications: because black seed lowers blood sugar and blood pressure, it can add to the effect of drugs that do the same. It may also slow blood clotting, so stop it before surgery and take care with blood thinners. Pregnancy: food amounts are fine, but avoid supplement doses. Skin: the oil occasionally causes a rash where it's applied.

Gelatin Chicken Soup (broth from feet and necks)

SoupChickenGlycineMake-ahead

Classic chicken soup built on a broth that sets like jelly. The broth takes a few hours in a pressure cooker, and the soup takes one more.

From Cooking with Chris (@coookwithchris). Where he gives no amount, I filled one in and said so. The nutrition numbers and the notes are mine.

What makes it work

  • Chicken feet and necks are mostly collagen
  • Blanch the bones first. Roasting is optional.
  • Use only enough water to cover
  • Make the broth separately, then build the soup on it

Good to know

  • Set broth is still mostly water. "Pure gelatin" is a stretch.
  • The vinegar barely changes the minerals

Ingredients

Broth (~8 cups)

  • 2 lbchicken feet
  • 2 lbchicken necks (or a carcass, wings, any bones)
  • 1 tbspapple cider vinegar
  • any mixonion (skin on), carrot, celery, leek, parsley, bay leaves, garlic, peppercorns
  • to covercold water, and no more

Soup (8 bowls)

  • 8 cupsthe broth
  • 4 lbchicken: boneless thighs, breast, a whole bird or bone-in pieces
  • 1 cupdiced carrots
  • 1 cupdiced celery
  • 1 cupdiced yellow onion
  • 3 clovesgarlic, minced
  • handfulfresh dill
  • drizzleoil (olive oil in the numbers)
  • to tastesalt and pepper (1½ tsp salt in the numbers)
  • optionalbrown rice pasta or egg noodles

Method

The broth

  1. Blanch. Cover the feet and necks with cold water, bring to a boil, boil 15 minutes and drain.
  2. Roast (optional). Roast at 450 °F for 15–20 minutes for color and a deeper flavor.
  3. Cook. Put the bones, aromatics and vinegar in a pressure cooker, slow cooker or stockpot. Add water just to cover. Too much water makes the broth weaker, not bigger. Pressure-cook on high for at least 2 hours, ideally 3–4. In a slow cooker or on the stove, go at least 8 hours and up to 24.
  4. Strain. The broth should set firm when it's cold. That's your soup base.

The soup

  1. Sweat the vegetables. Heat a drizzle of oil in a large pot over medium heat. Add the onion, carrots and celery with a pinch of salt and cook until they start to soften, 5–6 minutes. Add the garlic for 1 more minute.
  2. Simmer. Add the broth, dill and chicken. Turn the heat to medium-low and simmer until the chicken shreds easily, about 45 minutes. Bone-in chicken takes longer.
  3. Shred and season. Lift out the chicken, shred it into bite-sized pieces and return it to the pot. Season with salt and pepper. Serve as is or over noodles. You can skip the separate broth and simmer a whole chicken in water instead. It still pulls some gelatin out, but nothing like broth made from feet.

Gelatin-rich, not pure gelatin

Feet and necks are the right choice. Chicken feet are mostly skin, tendon and cartilage, so most of their protein is collagen. A broth that sets firm in the fridge has clearly extracted a lot of it. But even a firm gel is mostly water: commercial gelatin sets at a few percent by weight. When broths have been measured, home-made beat commercial, café broths were richest, and recipes that weren't standardized varied widely.[1] None reached the 20 g collagen doses used in research.

The firm set tells you this batch is at the good end. A bowl here also brings 40-plus grams of protein from the chicken, so the soup as a whole is filling and complete either way. For the glycine-to-methionine logic of pairing broth with meat, see the pho notes. (Moderate: direct measurements, one lab)

The vinegar: real chemistry, small payoff

Acid dissolves bone mineral. In a controlled extraction study, dropping broth pH from 8.4 to 5.3 raised calcium and magnesium extraction about 17- and 15-fold.[2] But the same study found that ordinary home and commercial broths had under 5% of the daily calcium or magnesium per serving. A tablespoon of vinegar in a full pot is probably a much milder acid than pH 5.3. So expect a modest gain from a very low base. The vinegar is harmless and worth keeping, but this isn't a mineral food. (Moderate for the extraction data. Inferred for how much 1 tbsp changes the pot's pH.)

Histamine: freeze it

A reply asked about histamine, and Chris said it's only an issue with histamine intolerance and suggested pressure-cooking and freezing. I found no published measurements of histamine in home bone broth. What is well established, mostly from fish, is how histamine forms: bacteria break down the amino acid histidine during storage, and cooking doesn't destroy histamine once it's made. A long simmer followed by days in the fridge gives bacteria more time. A pressure cooker followed by freezing gives them less. If broth or leftovers bother you, histamine is a plausible reason, and freezing in portions is the fix. (Inferred: food-microbiology principle, no broth-specific data)

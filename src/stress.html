Stress & Cortisol

Health GoalHPA axisSleepEnergy

Cortisol isn't the enemy. It's the hormone that gets you up in the morning and should fade by night. "Wired and tired" is usually that daily rhythm going flat, not adrenal glands wearing out, and the rhythm is set by light, sleep, food and salt.

A calmer day

  • Outside within an hour of waking, 10–30 minutes, no sunglasses
  • Same wake time every day, weekends too
  • Salt food to taste: about 1¼–2¼ tsp of salt a day, counting what's already in food
  • Three real meals with protein. No skipping breakfast on hard training days.
  • Coffee before noon. Tea with theanine after.
  • 200–400 mg magnesium glycinate and dim lights after dark

Watch out

  • Skip "adrenal support" pills. Lab tests found real thyroid and steroid hormones in them.
  • Licorice root raises blood pressure and drains potassium
  • Salt craving with darkening skin, weight loss and dizziness can be Addison's disease. See a doctor.
  • Don't stop prescribed steroids on your own. Stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

How it fits together

When you're stressed, the brain's hypothalamus signals the pituitary, the pituitary sends ACTH, and the adrenal glands on top of the kidneys make cortisol. Cortisol raises blood sugar, sharpens attention and holds back inflammation, and then tells the brain to stop. That loop is called the HPA axis. It runs on a 24-hour clock, high in the morning and low at night. Trouble starts when the clock loses its shape, the brake stops working or the glands run short of what they use.

The glands rarely fail. The rhythm, the brake and the supplies are where things go wrong.

First: "adrenal fatigue" isn't the problem

The idea that stress wears out the adrenal glands until they can't make enough cortisol doesn't hold up. A systematic review found no consistent link between "adrenal fatigue" symptoms and abnormal adrenal hormones. When the glands really do fail, as in Addison's disease, it's a serious autoimmune condition with low blood pressure, weight loss, salt craving and darkening skin, and a blood test finds it.

The symptoms people mean are real: tired in the morning, wired at night, crashing in the afternoon, craving salt. They're usually a disrupted rhythm, short sleep, under-eating or low sodium, or something else on the list below, like low thyroid or low iron. Those are all fixable.

Bright light in the morning, dim light at night

The master clock in the brain sits right above the optic nerves and resets from light hitting the eyes. Bright morning light anchors the cortisol peak to waking, and darkness lets melatonin rise at night. Indoor light is a small fraction of daylight, even on a cloudy day, so a day spent inside under bright evening screens gives the clock a weak signal at both ends. That's the flattened curve.

How: get outside within an hour of waking for 10–30 minutes (longer if it's overcast), without sunglasses. After dark, use lamps instead of overhead lights and turn screens down.

Sleep enough, at the same time each night

In a Chicago lab study, young men kept to a normal schedule, a short night or no sleep at all. After sleep loss, their cortisol was higher the next evening, when it should have been at its lowest. Higher evening cortisol then makes it harder to sleep, so the problem feeds itself. A fixed wake time does more to hold the rhythm than a fixed bedtime, because it pins the morning light and the cortisol peak in place.

How: 7–9 hours, same wake time every day. Keep the room cool and dark. For help falling asleep, try ¾ tsp (3 g) of glycine, chamomile tea or magnesium an hour before bed.

Salt your food

When sodium runs low, the kidneys release renin, which leads to aldosterone, the adrenal hormone that holds on to salt. A Cochrane review of 82 trials found that cutting sodium reliably raised renin, aldosterone and noradrenaline. Noradrenaline is the fight-or-flight signal. So a low-salt diet keeps part of the stress system switched on all the time. People who cook at home and avoid processed food often eat less sodium than they think.

Salt craving, feeling dizzy when you stand up and getting light-headed in the heat are often just low sodium. If salting food fixes them, that was the answer.

How: about 3–5 g of sodium a day. That's 1¼–2¼ tsp of salt counting what's already in food, and more if you sweat or train hard. Pair it with potassium foods: potatoes, avocado, orange juice.

Eat enough

One of cortisol's jobs is to keep blood sugar up when food is short, so going without food calls it in. In a randomised trial, 121 women were assigned to restrict calories to 1,200 a day, track calories, both or neither for three weeks. Restricting calories raised total cortisol output, and tracking calories raised how stressed people felt. Long fasts, very-low-carb diets and hard training on little food stack the same stress on top of each other.

That doesn't make fasting wrong. It means that if you're already short on sleep and feeling wired, under-eating is one more load on the same system.

How: three real meals with protein. Eat after hard training. If you sleep badly on very low carb, try some starch at dinner, such as potatoes or rice.

Get plenty of vitamin C

The adrenal glands hold some of the highest vitamin C levels in the body, and ACTH makes them release it into the blood as part of the stress response. The adrenal medulla uses it to make noradrenaline. In a German trial, 120 young adults took 3,000 mg of slow-release vitamin C a day or placebo for two weeks, then had to give a speech and do mental arithmetic in front of judges. The vitamin C group had a smaller jump in blood pressure, felt less stressed and recovered their cortisol faster. That dose is above the 2,000 mg upper limit, so it's a direction, not a dose to copy.

How: vitamin C foods every day: red peppers, kiwi, oranges, black currants. In a hard week, add 500–1,000 mg as acerola or plain ascorbic acid.

Take magnesium in the evening

Magnesium calms nerve firing by blocking one of the main excitatory receptors (NMDA) and helping GABA work. In a double-blind trial, 46 older adults with insomnia took 500 mg a day for 8 weeks. They fell asleep faster and slept better, their melatonin rose and their cortisol fell. Stress also makes you lose more magnesium in urine, so low intake and high stress feed each other. The benefit is clearest in people who start low.

How: 200–400 mg of magnesium glycinate in the evening. The glycine helps sleep too. Food sources are pumpkin seeds, spinach, avocado and dark chocolate.

Train hard, then recover

Hard exercise and cold water both spike cortisol and adrenaline, and that's the point. Repeated short, sharp stress with recovery afterwards trains the system to respond and then shut off. In a 12-week cold-water trial, the cortisol jump after each session got smaller as people adapted. Fit people also show smaller heart-rate and cortisol responses to mental stress.

The trouble is stress that never ends: hard training every day, on too little food and sleep. Then the brake stops working. Feeling wired, sleeping badly, a resting heart rate creeping up and weights going down are signs you're over-reaching.

How: two hard sessions a week (intervals or heavy lifting), easy walking on the other days, and at least one full rest day. A short cold plunge is optional. Don't do it late at night.

Cap coffee, and add theanine

Caffeine raises cortisol. In a four-week trial, regular coffee drinkers lost the cortisol response to their first cup of the day, but cortisol still rose with later cups through the afternoon and early evening, when it should be falling. Cutting coffee after noon protects both your sleep and the evening drop.

L-theanine, the amino acid in tea, takes the edge off. In small trials, 200 mg before a stressful task blunted the rise in heart rate, and four weeks of it improved anxiety and sleep scores. It goes well with caffeine: alert, without the jitter.

How: coffee before noon. After that, tea, or 100–200 mg of theanine. A cup of tea has only 10–25 mg, so stress doses come from capsules.

Worth considering

  • Glycine before bed. ¾ tsp (3 g) lowers core temperature, which is a signal for deep sleep. People felt sharper the next day in placebo-controlled trials. Glycine
  • Chamomile. Apigenin acts gently on the same brain receptor as anti-anxiety drugs. Extract trials helped generalised anxiety. Chamomile
  • Chinese skullcap. Calms anxiety in animal work through GABA. Some extracts are stimulating instead, depending on which compounds dominate. Skullcap
  • Ashwagandha. Trials report lower cortisol and stress scores, but most were run by the companies that sell the extracts. Rare liver injury has been reported, and it can raise thyroid hormone. Not yet written up on this site.
  • Slow breathing. Long exhales, about six breaths a minute, slow the heart through the vagus nerve within minutes. It's free and can't hurt.

Skip, or don't count on

  • "Adrenal support" supplementsMayo Clinic tested 12 of them. Every one contained the active thyroid hormone T3. Several also contained steroids, including budesonide, a prescription steroid drug. Taking hidden hormones can switch your own production off.
  • Saliva cortisol panels sold to diagnose "adrenal fatigue"A few samples on one day can't diagnose anything, and the condition they test for isn't real. Adrenal disease is diagnosed with blood tests.
  • Licorice root "for the adrenals"Glycyrrhizin stops the kidney from inactivating cortisol, so cortisol acts like aldosterone. Blood pressure goes up and potassium drops. Deglycyrrhizinated (DGL) licorice doesn't do this, and doesn't do the adrenal thing either.
  • Pantothenic acid (B5) megadosesB5 is in the cortisol-making pathway, but deficiency has only ever been produced on purpose in experiments. Extra B5 has no human cortisol data. B5

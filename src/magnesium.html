How to take it

  • Aim for 300–420 mg a day from food plus supplements
  • Glycinate for daily use: well absorbed and easy on the gut
  • Citrate if you also want it to act as a laxative
  • Skip oxide, which is poorly absorbed
  • Foods: pumpkin seeds, spinach, avocado, dark chocolate

The Research

Mechanisms, studies and evidence grades for each claim above.

Powers every cell

ATP must bind magnesium to form Mg-ATP, the biologically active form. Without magnesium, cellular energy use is impaired — magnesium is required for energy metabolism, not just supportive of it. It is a cofactor in over 300 enzymatic reactions, particularly in energy metabolism, neuromuscular function, and cellular signaling.1

Calmer mind, better sleep

Regulates neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity; supports GABA activity for a calming effect. Low magnesium is linked to increased neuronal excitability.2

Supports relaxation via GABA and nervous system regulation; associated with improved sleep quality, though the evidence is mixed-but-supportive rather than settled.3 (Moderate)

↑ back to summary

Relaxes muscles

Required for muscle relaxation (it counterbalances calcium) and helps prevent cramps and spasms. More broadly, it regulates ion channels (Ca²⁺, Na⁺, K⁺) and stabilizes membrane potential — see Calcium, Sodium, Potassium.

Steady heart rhythm

Regulates cardiac rhythm and electrical stability; imbalance can contribute to arrhythmias.1

Better blood sugar control

Required for insulin receptor signaling (tyrosine kinase activity).4 Low magnesium is associated with increased insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes risk,5 and supplementation can improve insulin sensitivity and glucose control.6 Magnesium-dependent ATP is required for glucose metabolism generally. (Moderate — associational + some interventional support)

Calms inflammation

Helps regulate inflammation and oxidative stress.2

Forms & intake

Roughly 300–420 mg/day, varying by sex and age.1 Cannot be synthesized — must come from diet. Bioavailability varies significantly by form; organic salts (citrate, glycinate) are generally better absorbed than inorganic forms like oxide.

  • Magnesium glycinate (bisglycinate) — chelated with glycine for improved absorption and less laxative effect; may support nervous system/sleep via the glycine component itself (see Glycine / Collagen). This is the form the Metabolic Health hub's Tier 1 supplement entry specifically recommends, for absorption and GI tolerance.
  • Magnesium citrate — relatively high absorption; osmotic activity draws water into the intestines (laxative effect); used for constipation and general supplementation.
  • Magnesium oxide — high elemental magnesium content but poor bioavailability; primarily acts as a laxative/antacid.
  • Magnesium threonate — proposed to cross the blood-brain barrier more effectively; limited human evidence, mostly preclinical. (Animal–in-vitro)
  • Magnesium malate — bound to malic acid (a TCA-cycle intermediate); sometimes used for fatigue, but evidence is limited.

Deficiency & safety

Neuromuscular: cramps, tremors. Cardiovascular: arrhythmias. CNS: anxiety, poor sleep.1

Caution: the kidneys are the main route for clearing excess magnesium, so anyone with reduced kidney function should clear supplementation with a doctor. In people with healthy kidneys, the usual limiting side effect is loose stools from the citrate and oxide forms.

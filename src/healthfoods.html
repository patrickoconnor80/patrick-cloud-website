Health Foods

Juices, teas and one dessert: things you'd eat or drink anyway, checked for what they actually do.

These entries used to sit on the Supplements index. They moved here because nobody takes a glass of orange juice or a bowl of ice cream the way they take a capsule. A food comes as a serving with calories, sugar and taste attached, and the useful question is whether it earns a regular place in the kitchen. Supplements keeps the capsules, powders, extracts and herbs. Nutrients covers what the body requires.

Same evidence grading as the rest of the series: Strong / Moderate / Animal–in-vitro / Inferred / Contested / Speculative. Foods have one advantage over supplements here: people have eaten them for a long time in large cohorts, so there is usually some human data. They have one disadvantage too: most of the trials were paid for by whoever sells the food, and each page names the funder where that applies.

Notes on this draft

Nine entries. Eight moved from Supplements in October 2026 and one is new. Beet Juice has the strongest and most direct human trial support. Pomegranate is the odd case where the mechanism is better established than the clinical literature, which is largely industry-funded and subject to an adverse FTC ruling on its advertising. Citrus Peel has an interesting mechanism and a dose gap wide enough that the human relevance is unresolved. The teas are split by compound class, like beets and citrus: oxidized theaflavins in Assam, microbially fermented theabrownins in pu-erh, intact catechins (EGCG) in green tea. Chamomile is here as a tea, though its sleep trials mostly used extracts.

Two entries are adjudications of posts on X rather than evaluations from scratch. Orange Juice settles an argument between two loud public positions. Green Tea came from a Siim Land thread whose numbers are mostly right in direction and small in size. Ice Cream is a plain summary of what holds up about dairy fat: the diabetes association in blood-marker cohorts is the human evidence, and the C15:0 and vaccenic acid findings are still animal and cell work.

What separates this index from Supplements is form, not evidence. A food here can still be graded weak, and freeze-dried oyster and acerola stay on Supplements because they come as capsules and powder even though they are whole foods.

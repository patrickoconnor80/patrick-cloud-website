Calcium

MineralFoundational

More than a bone mineral: a tightly controlled signal that runs muscles, nerves and clotting.

At a glance

Essential · food first
  • Builds strong bones Combines with phosphate to form the mineral that makes bone rigid. why ↓
  • Makes muscles contract Calcium flooding in is the trigger that makes muscle fibers pull. why ↓
  • Lets nerves fire Triggers the release of neurotransmitters. why ↓
  • Helps blood clot A required partner for several clotting factors. why ↓
Strong human evidence

How to take it

  • About 1,000 mg a day (1,200 mg for older adults)
  • Food first: milk, yogurt, cheese
  • Absorption depends on vitamin D and magnesium, not just intake

Watch out

  • Low vitamin D or magnesium causes problems even with enough calcium

Signs you're low

  • Cramps, twitching, tingling
  • Bone loss over time

Core mechanism

Ca²⁺ gradients enable rapid cellular signaling; systemic levels are maintained by PTH and vitamin D regulating absorption, storage, and excretion.1

↑ back to summary

Key functions

1. Structural mineral (bone)

Forms hydroxyapatite with phosphate, giving bone its rigidity.1 See Phosphate.

2. Muscle contraction

Ca²⁺ binds troponin, enabling the actin–myosin interaction that produces contraction.2

3. Neurotransmission

Ca²⁺ influx triggers vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release.3

4. Intracellular signaling

Acts as a second messenger for enzyme activation, hormone signaling, and broader cellular responses.

5. Blood coagulation

Required cofactor for multiple clotting factors.4

↑ back to summary

Regulation

Hormonal control. PTH increases bone resorption and renal Ca²⁺ reabsorption. Vitamin D (as calcitriol) increases intestinal Ca²⁺ absorption.5 Together they maintain a narrow serum Ca²⁺ range — see Vitamin D.

Electrolyte control. Magnesium is required for PTH secretion and signaling;6 phosphate couples with Ca²⁺ for both structure and energy. See Magnesium.

↑ back to summary

System interactions

  • Calcium system — core signaling ion driving contraction and neurotransmission
  • Energy system — Ca²⁺ activates PDH and TCA-cycle enzymes, linking demand to ATP production
  • Redox system — excess Ca²⁺ can increase mitochondrial stress in a context-dependent way
  • Structural system — bone acts as a reservoir buffering serum Ca²⁺
↑ back to summary

Failure points

  • ↓ Vitamin D → ↓ absorption → ↑ PTH
  • ↓ Magnesium → impaired PTH signaling
  • Dysregulated PTH → bone resorption or hypocalcemia
  • Mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload → dysfunction
↑ back to summary

Deficiency effects

Core disruption: insufficient available Ca²⁺ or impaired regulation leads to signaling and structural instability. Functional consequences include muscle cramps and tetany, impaired contraction, neurological excitability, and (chronically) bone demineralization. Low intake over time raises osteoporosis risk; severe hypocalcemia causes tingling, muscle cramps, and tetany.

Intake

RDA roughly 1,000 mg/day for most adults, ~1,200 mg/day for older adults and some other groups. Best food-first when possible.

↑ back to summary

Key insight

Calcium is not just a structural mineral — it's a tightly controlled signaling ion, and its function depends more on regulation (PTH, vitamin D, Mg²⁺) than on intake alone.

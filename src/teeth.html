Teeth & Gums

Health GoalCavitiesGum diseaseOral microbiome

A cavity has two causes. Bacteria make acid, and enamel loses minerals faster than saliva can put them back. Gum disease is an inflammation problem that reaches the rest of the body. Most of the fix is unglamorous: keep your mouth wet, clean it gently, limit how often acid hits it, and stop killing the bacteria you need.

A healthy-mouth week

  • Brush twice with an extra-soft brush and hydroxyapatite paste
  • Floss or use interdental brushes 3+ times a week
  • Scrape your tongue at night
  • Xylitol gum or mints after meals
  • Acidic or sweet drinks with meals, not sipped all day. Rinse with water after.
  • Breathe through your nose, day and night
  • Cleanings twice a year

Watch out

  • Antihistamines, antidepressants, ADHD and blood-pressure drugs often dry the mouth
  • Xylitol is poisonous to dogs. Keep it away from them.
  • Reflux bathes teeth in stomach acid, especially at night
  • Facial swelling from a bad tooth is an emergency
  • Before big treatment, ask "what's the diagnosis?" and get a second opinion

How it fits together

Enamel is about 96% mineral (hydroxyapatite), with no cells and no blood supply. It can't heal like skin. It can only gain and lose minerals at its surface. Every time the mouth's pH drops below about 5.5, minerals dissolve out. Saliva then neutralises the acid and puts calcium and phosphate back. A cavity is what happens when the losses outrun the repairs for long enough to break through. Early on, a weak chalky spot can still be remineralised. Once there's a hole, it needs a dentist. Gum disease runs on a different track: plaque that sits along the gumline inflames it, the gums bleed, and bacteria and inflammatory signals get into the bloodstream.

Every dental problem is microbial, chemical (acid and abrasion) or functional (dry mouth, airway, bite). The worst mouths have all three.

Keep your mouth wet

Saliva is the mouth's repair system. It buffers acid with bicarbonate and phosphate, carries the calcium and phosphate that rebuild enamel, clears food, and carries antibodies and antimicrobial proteins. When it stops, as after head-and-neck radiation or in Sjögren's syndrome, teeth can decay rapidly. Everyday dry mouth comes from mouth breathing (especially at night), antihistamines, antidepressants, ADHD drugs, some blood-pressure drugs, dry winter air and, in women around menopause, falling estrogen. Waking up with a dry mouth means the mouth was acidic all night.

How: breathe through your nose. If your nose is blocked, get it treated. Run a humidifier in winter. Review your medicines with your doctor. Chew xylitol gum to get saliva flowing. Snoring with a dry mouth calls for a sleep study.

Limit how often acid and sugar hit

Each sugar or acid hit drops the pH for roughly 20–30 minutes before saliva brings it back. The total time spent below pH 5.5 is what dissolves enamel, so how often you're exposed matters more than how much. One sweet drink sipped over 3 hours does more damage than the same drink finished with a meal. Plain acid erodes enamel even without bacteria: soda, sports drinks, kombucha, lemon water and vinegar shots all sit around pH 2–3.5. pH is logarithmic, so pH 3 is 100 times more acidic than pH 5. Black coffee is about pH 5, and with milk it's nearly neutral.

How: have sweet and acidic drinks with meals, through a straw, and rinse with water afterwards. Finish a meal with cheese or milk, since the calcium buffers the acid. Wait 30 minutes after acid before brushing. Choose a mineral water around pH 6, like Gerolsteiner, over a sweet drink. If you drink juice, the Orange Juice entry covers how to take it.

Brush gently, clean between teeth

Plaque is an organised biofilm, not leftover food, and rinsing doesn't remove it. Physically disrupting it does. In the Cochrane review of 35 trials, adding floss or interdental brushes to toothbrushing reduced gum inflammation more than brushing alone. Interdental brushes and water flossers did at least as well as floss. More is not better, though. Hard bristles and gritty "whitening" pastes wear enamel and push the gums back. A paste's abrasiveness is listed as its RDA score.

How: brush twice a day for 2 minutes with an extra-soft brush, angled into the gumline, using a paste with a low RDA (under about 70). Floss, or use interdental brushes or a water flosser, at least 3 times a week. Get a professional cleaning twice a year.

Treat bleeding gums

Bleeding gums are an open wound. Every time you brush or chew, oral bacteria and inflammatory signals (IL-6, TNF-α) enter the blood. Gum bacteria such as P. gingivalis have been found in artery plaque and in brain tissue. The clearest test of whether it matters comes from diabetes. In the Cochrane review of 35 trials, treating gum disease (deep cleaning) lowered A1c by about 0.43% after 3–4 months, similar to adding a second diabetes drug. The sulfur smell of gum disease is distinctive. Once you can recognise it, you'll notice it everywhere.

How: if your gums bleed, clean them gently every day for 2 weeks. Healthy gums stop bleeding. If they don't, get a periodontal exam: pockets of 1–3 mm are healthy, and 4 mm or more needs treatment. If you have diabetes, prediabetes or heart disease, put this near the top of your list.

Ditch antiseptic mouthwash

Bacteria on the tongue turn nitrate from vegetables into nitrite, which the body turns into nitric oxide, the molecule that relaxes blood vessels. In a London study, a week of chlorhexidine mouthwash cut the mouth's nitrite production by 90% and blood nitrite by 25%, starting within a day. Alcohol mouthwashes such as Listerine also dry the mouth, and over time the bad-breath bacteria come back while the helpful ones stay suppressed. A dentist-prescribed antiseptic has a place for short-term infection control. As a daily habit, it does more harm than good.

How: stop the daily antiseptic rinse. For fresh breath, scrape your tongue. After acidic food, rinse with water or with ½ tsp of baking soda in a glass of water.

Use hydroxyapatite toothpaste

Hydroxyapatite (HA) is the mineral enamel is made of. In toothpaste it fills early weak spots, binds to the surface, and plugs the open tubules in exposed dentin that cause sensitivity. Sensitivity is often the first thing people notice improving. Japan approved it as an anti-cavity ingredient in 1993. In an 18-month trial in 189 adults, an HA toothpaste prevented new cavities as well as a fluoride one: 89% versus 87% of people had no new decay. A 1-year trial in young children found the same. The EU's safety committee has cleared rod-shaped nano-HA for toothpaste. Swallowed, it dissolves into calcium and phosphate.

Fluoride: it does work. It converts the enamel surface into fluorapatite, which resists acid down to about pH 4.5 instead of 5.5. The benefit comes from contact with the tooth, so toothpaste does the job. Swallowing it is another matter. In a 2025 meta-analysis of 74 studies, higher fluoride exposure was linked to lower IQ in children. The link was clear above 1.5 mg/L in drinking water, and US tap water targets 0.7 mg/L. Below 1.5 mg/L the pooled result was null, but the low-bias studies still pointed lower. With HA working as well, you don't need fluoride. If you're getting a lot of cavities while you sort out your diet and dry mouth, a fluoride paste for a while is reasonable.

How: an HA toothpaste (about 10% or more), twice a day. Spit, don't rinse, so it stays on the teeth.

Chew xylitol gum

Xylitol is a sugar alcohol that cavity bacteria take in but can't ferment, so it doesn't feed them and it puts S. mutans under pressure. Chewing also gets saliva flowing. The trial evidence is weaker than its reputation. In the Cochrane review, adding xylitol to a fluoride toothpaste cut cavities about 13%, and the evidence was rated low quality. A 2024 Cleveland Clinic study found that a xylitol drink containing 30 g raised blood levels and made platelets stickier, and that higher blood xylitol was linked to heart events. A piece of gum has about 1 g.

How: one or two pieces of xylitol gum or mints after meals, adding up to a few grams a day. Don't drink large amounts. Too much can loosen your stools, and it's toxic to dogs.

Feed your gums

A review of nutrition and oral disease concluded that bleeding gums are a sensitive early sign of a poor diet: too much refined carbohydrate and polyunsaturated (seed) oil, too little protein, vitamin C or B12. Vitamin C is needed to make the collagen in gum tissue, and the gums are often the first place a shortfall shows. Vitamin D runs calcium absorption and the mouth's antimicrobial proteins. Low D is consistently linked with more cavities, and severe deficiency (rickets) damages teeth as they form. K2 helps move calcium into bone, including the jawbone. None of these can fix teeth that are bathed in acid all day.

How: vitamin C from citrus, kiwi or peppers (rinse after citrus), vitamin D from sun, K2 from aged cheese and egg yolks, protein at each meal, and little or no seed oil. Teeth form in childhood, so a mother's and child's vitamin D status matters most then.

Scrape your tongue

Bad breath comes mostly from volatile sulfur compounds made by bacteria in the grooves at the back of the tongue, and from gum pockets. A tongue scraper removes that coating physically, which rinsing can't do. A thin white coating is normal. A thick, smelly one, or breath that smells sulfurous, means gum disease, dry mouth or tonsil stones are worth checking. The catch: the tongue also hosts the nitrate-reducing bacteria, and heavy scraping can reduce them.

How: scrape lightly, back to front, 3–5 strokes at night. Leave a few hours between scraping and arugula or beets if you're eating them for blood flow.

Worth considering

  • Feed the good bacteria. Oral probiotics shift the microbiome only while you take them. Changing the conditions lasts longer: nitrate-rich greens, polyphenols such as green tea, less frequent sugar and xylitol. Green Tea
  • Mouth tape at night. A thin strip keeps you breathing through your nose. Only use it if your nose is clear, and get a sleep study first if you snore loudly or stop breathing in your sleep.
  • Early cavities can be reversed. A chalky white spot that hasn't broken through can remineralise with the steps above. Ask your dentist about the P11-4 peptide (Curodont) for early lesions before drilling.
  • Kids: chewy food and nasal breathing. Jaws grow to the work they're given. Treating allergies and blocked noses early prevents a mouth-breathing habit that affects how the face develops. Advice like this is for growing children. It won't change an adult's face.

Skip, or don't count on

  • Whitening toothpasteToo abrasive to be good for enamel and too weak to whiten. Peroxide needs to be a gel held on the teeth. Get a cleaning first, because whitening doesn't work through plaque.
  • Charcoal and clay tooth powdersCharcoal is abrasive, and bentonite clay is often contaminated with heavy metals. Natural doesn't mean safe.
  • Oil pulling instead of treatment20 minutes twice a day for 40 days gave a small drop in bacteria, with little change in plaque or gum inflammation. It won't remineralise anything, and it can't fix a real cavity.
  • "Heal your cavities" protocolsRaw milk rinses, megadoses of vitamins and secret powders can't regrow enamel once there's a hole. A cavity left alone can become an abscess.
  • Lemon water and apple cider vinegarDrunk daily, they're two of the most erosive habits in wellness. If you have them, use a straw and rinse after.
  • Peroxide rinsesThey kill bacteria broadly and do little to whiten.

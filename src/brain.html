Brain & Focus

Health GoalFocusMemoryBrain fog

The brain is 2% of your weight and burns about 20% of your energy. Most brain fog is an energy problem, a building-material problem or a signalling problem, and each of those has specific nutrients behind it. Sleep and exercise come first, though.

A sharp week

  • 7–9 hours of sleep, fixed wake time, morning light
  • Walk daily, hard cardio twice a week, lift twice
  • 2–3 eggs a day, liver once a week
  • Wild salmon or sardines 2–3 times
  • 1 tsp creatine (5 g) a day
  • Morning coffee or tea, with 100–200 mg theanine for deep work

Watch out

  • Metformin, acid blockers and a plant-based diet all lower B12. Get it checked.
  • Snoring with daytime fog can be sleep apnoea
  • Sudden confusion, weakness or slurred speech is an emergency. Call for help.
  • Memory loss that's getting worse needs a doctor, not supplements

How it fits together

A thinking brain needs three things. Fuel: a steady supply of glucose (or ketones), and mitochondria and B vitamins to burn it. Building materials: DHA, choline and cholesterol for membranes, and B12 for the myelin that insulates nerves. Signals: neurotransmitters like dopamine, serotonin and acetylcholine, which are made from amino acids using B6, iron and choline. Sleep resets all three, and exercise grows the hardware.

A clear head, steady focus and memory that holds

Brain fog usually means one of the three is short. Find which before reaching for a nootropic.

Sleep

After one night of short sleep, attention lapses, reaction time and working memory all get measurably worse, and people consistently underrate how impaired they are. During deep sleep, the spaces between brain cells widen and fluid flushes out waste, including the amyloid linked to Alzheimer's. Memories from the day are replayed and stored during deep and REM sleep. No supplement makes up for losing it.

How: follow the Sleep guide: fixed wake time, morning light, no caffeine after noon, no late alcohol.

Do aerobic exercise

In a year-long trial, 120 older adults were assigned to brisk walking or stretching. In the walkers the hippocampus, the brain's memory centre, grew about 2%, roughly reversing a year or two of age-related shrinkage. In the stretchers it shrank. Exercise raises BDNF, a growth factor for neurons, and improves blood flow to the brain. One workout sharpens attention for a couple of hours afterwards.

How: walk daily, plus two harder cardio sessions a week. Try a short, brisk walk or a few minutes of hard effort before a big mental task.

Rule out low B12 and iron

B12 keeps myelin intact. Deficiency causes memory problems, low mood, numbness and, left long enough, permanent nerve damage, sometimes before anaemia shows. It's common in older adults, vegans, and people on metformin or acid blockers. Iron is needed to make dopamine and to carry oxygen to the brain. In a trial in teenage girls who were iron-deficient but not anaemic, iron improved verbal learning and memory. Low ferritin is common in women who menstruate.

How: ask for B12 (plus methylmalonic acid if it's borderline) and ferritin. Food sources: liver, red meat, shellfish, eggs and dairy. If you supplement iron, take it every other day with vitamin C.

Keep blood sugar steady

A big refined-carb meal spikes blood sugar, then over-corrects, and the dip a couple of hours later feels like fog and hunger. Over decades, insulin resistance does real damage. The brain becomes worse at using glucose, type 2 diabetes roughly doubles dementia risk, and some researchers call Alzheimer's "type 3 diabetes." Thiamine (B1) is the gate that lets glucose into the mitochondria, and heavy drinking depletes it.

How: protein and fat with every meal, refined starch kept small, a 10-minute walk after eating. Take the Metabolic Health basics seriously.

Eat wild oily fish

DHA makes up a large share of the fat in brain cell membranes, especially at synapses, and the body converts very little from the plant omega-3 in nuts and seeds. People who eat more fish tend to have slower cognitive decline. Fish-oil capsule trials in healthy adults are mixed, so food, starting early, looks better than capsules late. Cutting seed oils also matters, because linoleic acid competes with the omega-3s for space in membranes.

How: wild salmon (about 8 oz) or a can or two of sardines, 2–3 times a week. Fish roe and oysters also carry DHA.

Eat eggs and liver for choline

Choline is the raw material for acetylcholine, the neurotransmitter of memory and attention, and for phosphatidylcholine, a main membrane fat. The body makes some, but not enough, and most adults eat less than the recommended amount. Egg-avoidance advice made that worse. Low choline in pregnancy affects the baby's brain development.

How: about 425–550 mg a day. One egg has about 150 mg, so 3 eggs gets most of the way. 3 oz of beef liver has about 350 mg.

Take creatine

The brain runs its own creatine-phosphocreatine buffer to keep ATP up when demand jumps. In young vegetarians, 5 g a day for 6 weeks improved working memory and reasoning. A review of 6 trials found short-term memory and reasoning improved, with the benefit concentrated in vegetarians, older adults and people under stress. During sleep deprivation, a large single dose improved processing speed. Young meat-eaters who are well rested see little.

How: 1 tsp (5 g) of creatine monohydrate a day, any time. It's cheap, well studied and safe in healthy kidneys.

Pair caffeine with theanine

Caffeine blocks adenosine, the sleep-pressure signal, so it raises alertness, reaction time and focus. The cost is jitter and, later in the day, worse sleep. L-theanine, from tea, smooths the edge. In a crossover trial, 100 mg of theanine plus 50 mg of caffeine improved attention-switching and resisted distraction better than caffeine alone. Tea contains both naturally, but only 10–25 mg of theanine a cup.

How: coffee or tea in the morning, last one before noon. For deep work, add 100–200 mg of theanine at about a 2:1 ratio with caffeine.

Worth considering

  • Protect your hearing. In the Lancet dementia commission, untreated hearing loss in midlife is one of the largest modifiable risk factors for dementia. Use hearing protection, and get hearing aids if you need them.
  • Morning light. Sets the clock that times alertness, and brighter days lift mood. Sunlight
  • Cold water. A short cold plunge raises noradrenaline several-fold for an hour or two. That's a real alertness and mood bump. Cold Exposure
  • Magnesium. Calms nerve firing and helps sleep, especially if you're low. Magnesium

Skip, or don't count on

  • GinkgoIn a trial of over 3,000 older adults followed for about 6 years, ginkgo didn't prevent dementia or slow decline.
  • Nootropic stacksProprietary blends at doses too low to test anything. Racetams aren't approved supplements in the US.
  • Brain-training appsYou get better at the games. Gains rarely carry over to everyday thinking.
  • Fish-oil capsules instead of fishMixed trials in healthy adults. Eat the fish.
  • Lion's mane and other mushroom extractsInteresting in cells and mice. Human trials are tiny and short.

Glycine / Collagen

Amino Acid Foundational Structural
TL;DR: Glycine provides substrate for collagen and several metabolic pathways; collagen provides structural integrity.

Overview

Collagen is a structural protein built from glycine, proline, and hydroxyproline, arranged in a triple helix that provides tensile strength. The path runs glycine → procollagen → stable triple helix (via hydroxylation of proline and lysine, which requires vitamin C and iron) → mature collagen fiber (via lysyl oxidase cross-linking, which requires copper). See Collagen Synthesis Cofactors below for the citations on each step.

Intake

Estimated metabolic demand runs 12–15 g/day against endogenous production of roughly 3 g/day.1 (Inferred — modeled from turnover estimates, not a formal RDA) Collagen turnover is continuous and metabolically expensive, which is the basis for treating glycine as conditionally essential in humans rather than fully dispensable.

Functions

Structural

Contributes carbon and nitrogen to the purine ring (adenine, guanine).2 Major component of skin, tendons, ligaments, and bone, where it provides tensile strength.3

Functional

Glycine + succinyl-CoA is the first step in heme synthesis.2 Glycine conjugates bile acids, increasing their solubility and lipid absorption.4 Glycine + arginine forms creatine, supporting rapid ATP regeneration in muscle and brain.2

Protective

Glycine + cysteine (+ glutamate) form glutathione, which neutralizes reactive oxygen species and handles phase II detoxification, including protection against heavy metals and xenobiotics.5 Glycine availability can be rate-limiting for glutathione synthesis.6 Glycine also conjugates toxins directly (e.g., benzoate → hippurate) for urinary excretion.4

Systems

Heart. Improves insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis via enhanced insulin secretion;16 supports VEGF signaling involved in angiogenesis.17 (Moderate)

Brain. Blocks ROS formation in brain mitochondria;18 supports survival of a neural stem cell subpopulation;19 reduces damage from hypoxia/ischemia in rat models.20 Collagen VI has been reported to protect against amyloid-β toxicity, per a secondary summary of preclinical work.21 (Animal–in-vitro)

Gut. Collagen peptides reduce intestinal epithelial barrier dysfunction in vitro;22 glycine showed partial benefit for mucosal regeneration in a rat ischemia-reperfusion model.23 (Animal–in-vitro)

Sleep. Oral glycine raises extracellular serotonin in rat prefrontal cortex — the proposed route to improved sleep quality via the serotonin → melatonin pathway.24 (Inferred — mechanism is rodent data; human sleep-outcome studies exist for glycine but aren't yet linked into this write-up)

Longevity. Glycine suppresses methionine-induced hyperhomocysteinemia, offsetting a known cost of methionine overload;14 methionine restriction itself extends lifespan across multiple model organisms.13 (Animal–in-vitro)

Deficiency effects

Impaired collagen synthesis — compounded by low vitamin C or low protein intake, since both feed the same pathway.

Collagen synthesis cofactors

  • Vitamin C — required for prolyl/lysyl hydroxylase activity;9 reduces Fe³⁺→Fe²⁺ and Cu²⁺→Cu⁺ into their active enzymatic forms;1011 also removes an IGF-1 inhibition pathway, and IGF-1 itself stimulates collagen synthesis.78 (Strong)
  • Iron — Fe²⁺ hydroxylates proline and lysine so the triple helix can form.10
  • Copper — Cu⁺-dependent lysyl oxidase cross-links collagen fibers into stable covalent bonds.10
  • Sodium — SVCT1/2 transporters move vitamin C into tissue along sodium gradients, an indirect but relevant dependency.12 (Inferred)

Top foods

  • Bone broth — collagen/gelatin content varies with prep; sodium varies widely
  • Beef tendon — very collagen/gelatin-rich; needs long simmer/braise/pressure cook
  • Oxtail — connective-tissue-rich cut; braise or pressure cook
  • Beef shank — high in connective tissue; braises and stocks

Grey Hair & Hair Loss

Grey hair is a stem-cell problem, and baldness is mostly a hormone-sensitivity problem. Nutrition, thyroid and stress sit on top of both. When grey hair has come back in people, it's usually because a specific deficiency or disease was fixed. The antioxidant products have much weaker evidence than their marketing suggests.

Abstract

Hair gets its colour from melanocytes in the follicle bulb. Each hair cycle, those melanocytes are restocked from a pool of melanocyte stem cells. Greying happens when that pool runs out, stops working, or is killed off. Oxidative stress (hydrogen peroxide building up as catalase fails), sympathetic-nerve bursts during stress, and mitochondrial failure are all proven ways to deplete it in mice or in human follicles. Genes set the schedule. Deficiencies and disease can push it earlier, including low B12, low iron stores and hypothyroidism, and copper matters in theory. Those are also the cases where grey hair has actually come back.

Baldness is a separate problem. Pattern baldness (androgenetic alopecia) is follicles shrinking under DHT in people genetically sensitive to it. Diffuse shedding (telogen effluvium) is the form driven by nutrition, thyroid, stress and crash diets. The drugs work for pattern baldness. Nutrients work for shedding when something is actually missing. Most of the confusion comes from mixing up those two problems, and from treating product trials run by the product's maker as independent evidence.

The argument in brief

Seven parts

  1. Part IGreying is melanocyte stem-cell exhaustion. The pigment enzyme needs copper, but what fails first is the cells. Oxidative stress, stress-nerve bursts and broken mitochondria each deplete them in experiments.
  2. Part IIIn people, premature greying tracks with low B12, low ferritin, low vitamin D, low calcium, smoking, oxidative-stress markers and heart disease. The copper data are thinner and less consistent than usually claimed.
  3. Part IIIWhat has actually reversed grey hair, ranked: fixing a deficiency or disease, then certain drugs, then natural reversal, then sponsor-run topical trials. After that come old vitamin reports and mouse-only work.
  4. Part IVNutrients and compounds. Copper, B12, iron, vitamin D and thyroid hormone matter when they're low. NAC and luteolin protect pigment cells in mice. The human product trials are small and mostly run by the makers.
  5. Part VBaldness comes in two kinds. Pattern loss is DHT acting through dermal-papilla signals that oxidative stress and inflammation amplify, and early onset is a marker of insulin resistance and heart disease. Shedding is where nutrition matters.
  6. Part VIWhat works for hair loss, ranked by evidence, from the drugs down to the oils and supplements.
  7. Part VIIWhat to do. Test before you supplement, eat the copper and B12 foods, don't overdo zinc, vitamin A or selenium, and set expectations by how long the hair has been grey.

Hair is a readout of the terrain. The best-supported way to keep your colour or your hair is to find what's actually wrong. Stacking antioxidants hoping one hits is much weaker.

Reader's guide

Parts I–III build the model. Part IV goes through the nutrients and compounds one at a time. Parts V–VI cover baldness, and Part VII is the practical summary. Supplements and nutrients that already have their own entries on this site are linked where they come up.

PART I

How Hair Gets Its Colour, and Loses It

I.1The pigment line

Melanin is made from the amino acid tyrosine by tyrosinase, a copper enzyme, inside melanocytes that sit at the base of each growing hair. The melanocytes hand the pigment to the keratin cells building the shaft. Tyrosinase gets its copper from a copper pump (ATP7A, the Menkes protein) inside the cell. Without the pump, tyrosinase is made but stays inactive, which is why Menkes disease, a genetic copper-transport failure, produces pale, steely hair.3

tyrosine ──tyrosinase (Cu)──▶ DOPA ──▶ dopaquinone ──▶ eumelanin (brown/black) └──(+ cysteine)──▶ pheomelanin (red/yellow)

Pigment is only made while the hair is growing (anagen). When the follicle rests and restarts, it needs a fresh set of melanocytes, and those come from melanocyte stem cells parked higher up the follicle.

Strongtextbook pigment biology; the copper-pump requirement is shown directly in cells.

I.2Greying is the stem-cell pool running out

The landmark 2005 Science paper by Nishimura and colleagues showed, in mice and in ageing human follicles, that greying is caused by melanocyte stem cells failing to maintain themselves. They either die when they should go dormant, or they mature in the wrong place and are used up.1 A 2023 Nature paper from NYU added a twist. These stem cells normally shuttle between compartments of the follicle, and with age more of them get stuck in the wrong compartment where they can't be triggered to make pigment cells.2 Stuck isn't dead. That's one reason some grey hair can come back (Part III).

Strongfor stem-cell exhaustion as the core mechanism, shown in mice and confirmed in human follicles; Animal–in vitrofor the "stuck cells" model, which so far is mostly mouse work.

I.3What exhausts the pool

Oxidative stress. Making melanin generates hydrogen peroxide, so pigment cells live under constant oxidative load. A University of Bradford group found hydrogen peroxide at millimolar levels in grey and white hair shafts. They also found catalase (the enzyme that breaks it down) and the methionine-sulfoxide repair enzymes almost absent in grey follicles. The resulting oxidation of a methionine in tyrosinase's active site slows the enzyme down.4 A Charité group found melanocyte death, a marker of mitochondrial DNA damage, and more oxidative stress in greying follicles. Adding an oxidant to cultured pigmented follicles killed melanocytes.5 A Wuhan study, part-funded by L'Oréal, found catalase switched off in both the pigment cells and their stem cells in grey follicles.6

Stress nerves. Harvard's Hsu lab showed in 2020 that in mice, acute stress greys hair through the sympathetic nerves wired to the stem-cell niche, not through cortisol and not through the immune system. A burst of noradrenaline makes the stem cells all activate at once, so the reserve is spent and gone for good.7 The stressor in that study was an injected pain-causing chemical, which is intense and acute.

Mitochondria. Deleting a mitochondrial DNA-maintenance enzyme in mice raised oxidative stress in the stem cells and greyed the coat. The surviving melanocytes still made plenty of pigment. The problem was the number of cells, not their output.32 NAC's effect in these mice is in Part IV.

Genes. The first genome-wide hit for greying, in 6,000 Latin Americans, was near IRF4, a gene that regulates melanin production.9 Family history is the most consistent risk factor in every case-control study below.

Strongthat oxidative damage, stress-nerve activity and mitochondrial failure can each deplete the pool experimentally; Inferredthat these are the main drivers of ordinary age-related greying in a given person, rather than the genetic clock.

Two more pathways a heat-sensitive enzyme and the stress-hormone axis

A heat-sensitive tyrosinase. A common tyrosinase variant, R402Q (rs1126809), is carried by nearly half of people of European descent.11 It makes an enzyme that works normally at 31 °C but misfolds at body temperature and is trapped inside the cell instead of reaching the pigment granules.10 The variant is tied to lighter hair, skin and eyes. Whether it speeds greying hasn't been studied. One suggestion is that people with two copies may do better avoiding chronic heat, but that's untested.11

Cortisol suppressing the pigment hormones. ACTH and MSH, cut from the same precursor (POMC), drive pigmentation. Cortisol feeds back to suppress POMC, so chronically high cortisol could cut the pigment signal. MSH activation also needs vitamin C, copper, zinc and glycine.11 This is a different stress route from the nerve mechanism above, which worked without the adrenal glands.7 Both may be real.

The practical framing that follows is to find the weakest link and fix it. The candidates are energy signalling (thyroid, insulin sensitivity, protein, carbohydrate, stress), the energy machinery (B vitamins, iron, copper, magnesium) and antioxidant defence.11 Speculative

PART II

What Greying Is Linked To in People

Almost everything here comes from small case-control studies of premature greying (before about 20–30, depending on ethnicity), mostly from India, Iran and Egypt. They compare blood levels in people who grey early with people who don't.

These are cross-sectional snapshots of a few dozen people each, in populations where deficiency is common. They can't say whether low copper or low ferritin caused the greying, or whether both reflect something else like gut absorption, inflammation or diet quality. The copper case rests on one study where it was barely significant and one where it didn't track with severity. The pattern that does hold up across studies is broader than any one nutrient: people who grey early are, on average, less well nourished and less metabolically healthy. That fits the terrain framing of the Metabolic Health paper better than it fits a single-nutrient fix.

PART III

What Has Actually Reversed Grey Hair

Reviews of the field open the same way: grey hair is generally thought permanent, and there's no proven treatment other than dye.24,17 Reversal does happen, though, and where it's been documented tells you what's worth trying. Ranked by strength of evidence:

1 · Fix the causecase series, reviews

Treating the underlying deficiency or disease. The 2020 meta-analysis concluded that "treating the accompanying pathologies led to the reversal of the disease in many cases."16 Examples: premature greying reversed on oral iron in a Korean patient20. White hair darkened in two patients given T3 for hypothyroidism, one of them in myxoedema coma.21 B12 deficiency is on the meta-analysis's list of treatable causes.16

2 · Drugsincidental case reports

A systematic review found 27 reports of drug-induced repigmentation.23 Most were side effects of anti-inflammatories (thalidomide, adalimumab, prednisone, cyclosporine) or drugs that stimulate melanin (imatinib, erlotinib, tamoxifen, latanoprost, and levodopa, which darkened a Parkinson's patient's white hair25). None of these would be taken for grey hair. They point to inflammation and pigment signalling as levers.

3 · On its ownhuman hair mapping

Columbia's Picard lab mapped pigment along single hairs and found white hairs that regained colour naturally, across sexes, ethnicities and body sites. In some people it tracked with stressful periods and their end.8 Their model suggests hairs near the greying threshold can flip back when the load comes off. Hairs far past it can't. This is the most important finding for expectations: early grey is the grey most likely to come back.

4 · Topical productssponsor-run trials

Three trials: Givaudan's antioxidant ingredient (placebo-controlled; grey proportion down 17% vs 8% on placebo)28, and two single-arm, open-label trials by one Indian research company, one funded by the serum's manufacturer29,30. Details in Part IV.

5 · Old vitamin reportsuncontrolled, 1940s–80s

PABA at 12–24 g a day darkened grey hair in 5 of 20 patients taking it for other conditions26. Calcium pantothenate (B5) 200 mg plus plucking the grey hairs gave mixed results in 39 young women with no control group.27 The meta-analysis gives these a "low grade (2A)" recommendation.16

6 · Miceanimal only

Luteolin, oral or topical, slowed greying in a greying-prone mouse strain31. NAC in drinking water prevented greying in mice with broken mitochondria32. Neither has been tested in people.

Moderatethat correcting a real deficiency or thyroid disease can restore colour, based on case reports and the meta-analysis, not trials; Speculativethat any supplement reverses ordinary age-related greying in a well-nourished person.

PART IV

Nutrients and Compounds for Grey Hair

IV.1Copper

Tyrosinase can't make melanin without copper, delivered inside the cell by the ATP7A copper pump. When that delivery fails, as in Menkes disease, hair comes out pale.3 In one study, teenagers who greyed early had lower serum copper than controls.12 Getting enough matters. Beef liver and oysters are by far the densest food sources (see Copper and Freeze-Dried Oyster).

More than enough doesn't help. Free copper is itself pro-oxidant, which is the opposite of what a greying follicle needs.11 Long-term high-dose zinc drains copper. In mice, high-dose oral zinc lightened new hair and cut its melanin content.33 Injected copper peptides (GHK-Cu) are being tried for grey hair. They grow hair in mice, but there are no human studies on colour.37

Strongfor copper's role in pigment; Contestedthat low copper explains most premature greying (Part II).

IV.2B12, folate, iron and vitamin D

These are the deficiencies most consistently linked to early greying (Part II), and the ones where fixing a real shortfall has brought colour back. Premature greying reversed on oral iron in a reported case.20 B12 and folate deficiency are on the meta-analysis list of treatable causes.16 They are worth testing because they are cheap to fix and matter well beyond hair. See B12, Folate, Iron and Vitamin D.

Moderatecase reports and case-control data, in people who were actually deficient.

IV.3Thyroid hormone

An underactive thyroid is a recognised cause of early greying. White hair darkened in two patients treated with T3, one recovering from myxoedema coma and one given too much thyroid hormone.21 In cultured human follicles, T3 and T4 directly raised pigment production.22 In people with an underactive thyroid, two herbs raised T3 in small placebo-controlled trials:

  • black seed, about 1 scant tsp ground (2 g) a day for 8 weeks, in Hashimoto's34 (see Black Seed)
  • ashwagandha root extract, 600 mg a day, in subclinical hypothyroidism35

Neither herb has been studied for hair.

Moderatefor treating hypothyroidism restoring colour; Inferredthat thyroid support helps hair colour when the thyroid is already normal.

IV.4Antioxidants

NAC. In mice whose mitochondria were broken by deleting Dguok, NAC in drinking water (1 mg/mL from day 20 to day 70) restored the melanocyte stem cells and prevented most of the greying. Half that dose didn't work.32 By standard scaling, the effective dose works out to roughly 1 g a day for an adult. It was prevention, started before greying, in a genetic model. No human studies. See NAC.

Luteolin. A flavonoid found in celery, peppers and herbs. Fed at 0.5 mg/kg a day, or applied to the skin, it slowed greying in a mouse strain bred to grey early. It did this by keeping the follicle's stem-cell signalling (endothelin to its receptor) from fading with age. The work was part-funded by Hoyu, a Japanese hair-dye company. No human studies.31

Topical antioxidant serums. One placebo-controlled trial exists. In 44 men over four months, an antioxidant ingredient from Givaudan, which ran the trial, reduced the proportion of grey hair by 17% versus 8% on placebo.28 Two other serums reported less grey in open-label studies with no placebo arm. One was a multi-ingredient serum containing rosemary oil and the anti-grey ingredient MelanoGray, funded by its manufacturer.29,30

Diet. People who grey early have higher MDA, a marker of oxidised fat, and lower glutathione.15 Fewer seed oils (Linoleic Acid), enough protein, glycine and cysteine for glutathione (Glycine, Cysteine), and fruit all lower the oxidative load the follicle sits under.

Animal–in vitrofor NAC and luteolin; Moderatefor the one placebo-controlled topical trial, with the maker running it.

IV.5Vitamin B5 and PABA

Both come from an older literature. In 39 young women, calcium pantothenate (B5) 200 mg a day combined with plucking the grey hairs reduced grey counts in most of those followed long-term. There was no control group, and the authors credit the plucking as much as the vitamin.27 PABA darkened grey hair in 5 of 20 patients, but at 12–24 g a day taken as a drug for other diseases.26 A 2020 meta-analysis gives both a low-grade recommendation.16 See B5.

Speculativeuncontrolled reports.

IV.6Stress

Stress greys hair through the sympathetic nerves wired to the stem-cell niche.7 In people, grey hairs near the threshold have regained colour as stress eased.8 Of everything on this list, managing stress is the lever with direct evidence for reversal in ordinary people, without a deficiency to fix.

Moderatemouse mechanism plus human hair-mapping data.

PART V

Baldness: Two Different Problems

V.1Pattern loss vs. shedding

Androgenetic alopecia (AGA), meaning male-pattern or female-pattern hair loss, is follicles miniaturising over many cycles, driven by DHT in genetically sensitive scalp. Temple and crown in men, widening part in women. Each cycle the hair comes back thinner and shorter until it's vellus fuzz. The follicle is still there for years, which is why treatment works best early.38

Telogen effluvium (TE) is diffuse shedding, usually two to three months after a trigger: illness, surgery, childbirth, crash dieting, low protein, iron deficiency, thyroid disease, major stress. Many follicles switch to resting at once. It is usually reversible once the trigger is fixed.49 A small study of medical students before a major exam found stress shifted immune markers and hair-cycle parameters, though within the normal range.52

The distinction matters because the evidence splits cleanly. Nutrients fix shedding when something is missing. They don't reverse pattern loss. A 2024 systematic review found deficiencies in vitamin D, iron, zinc and B vitamins associated with AGA, but the evidence was "not entirely consistent."50 (Alopecia areata, patchy autoimmune loss, is a third condition and out of scope here.)

Strongfor the AGA/TE distinction and DHT's role in AGA.

V.2Early baldness as a metabolic marker

This is where hair connects to the rest of the series. A Finnish case-control study found men who started balding before 35 had a "strikingly increased risk" of hyperinsulinaemia and insulin-resistance disorders: obesity, hypertension and dyslipidaemia.53 A meta-analysis of 37,000 men found severe baldness carried a 32% higher risk of coronary heart disease, rising to 48% for severe vertex (crown) baldness.54 Frontal recession wasn't linked. Premature greying shows the same pattern with heart disease (Part II).

Moderateobservational, but consistent across cohorts; Inferredthat improving insulin sensitivity slows hair loss. That hasn't been tested directly.

Why this matters more than the hair

Early crown balding or early greying is worth reading as a prompt to check fasting insulin, lipids and blood pressure, the same terrain the Metabolic Health paper works on. The hair isn't the disease. It may be an early, visible readout of it.

V.3How DHT shrinks a follicle

DHT doesn't act on the cells that build the hair. It acts on androgen receptors in the dermal papilla, the small cluster of signalling cells at the base of the follicle that tells the hair-making matrix cells when to grow and when to stop. In balding scalp, DHT makes those papilla cells send out three "stop" signals:

  • TGF-β1, which ends the growth phase. With androgen receptors restored, androgen cut the growth of neighbouring keratinocytes by about half in co-culture.55
  • DKK-1, which blocks Wnt, the main "keep growing" signal. Blocking DKK-1 with an antibody reversed DHT's growth inhibition.56
  • IL-6, an inflammatory cytokine. It stunted hair-shaft growth in cultured human follicles, and injected into mice it pushed follicles into regression early.57

Balding scalp is also mildly inflamed. Gene-expression data from bald versus haired scalp of the same men show more T cells, mast cells and neutrophils in the bald patches.58 And in dermal papilla cells, DHT raises reactive oxygen species and damages mitochondria. Three different antioxidant approaches blunted this in cells or mice and lowered the DKK-1, TGF-β1 and IL-6 that DHT induces:

  • a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant (MitoQ)60
  • red light61
  • a melon-leaf compound, meloside A (tested by the company developing it)59
DHT ──androgen receptor (dermal papilla)──▶ ROS ↑ · mitochondria ↓ └──▶ TGF-β1 · DKK-1 · IL-6 ──▶ matrix cells stop dividing ──▶ shorter growth phase ──▶ thinner hair

So DHT is the trigger and oxidative stress and inflammation carry its signal. DHT is still upstream of all of it in genetically sensitive follicles, which is why lowering it with finasteride regrows hair.38 The downstream steps explain why red light, which has placebo-controlled trials, works, and why antioxidant and anti-inflammatory approaches are reasonable additions to the drugs rather than replacements.

Strongfor DHT acting through dermal papilla signals, replicated in human balding papilla cells; Animal–in vitrofor antioxidants blocking those signals. No human studies yet, apart from red light.

V.4Oxidised fats and the follicle

Linoleic acid, the main fat in seed oils, is easily oxidised into lipid hydroperoxides (see Linoleic Acid). Applied to mouse skin, linoleic-acid hydroperoxides pushed follicles into the regression phase early and caused cell death in the hair matrix. They also killed human skin keratinocytes in culture.62 Unoxidised linoleic acid did the opposite on dermal papilla cells: it switched on Wnt growth signalling and lowered DKK-1.63 The damage comes from the oxidation product, which is the same substrate-times-oxidant logic as the cardiovascular case. Linoleic acid is the raw material for that oxidation, and the more of it there is in tissue, the more peroxide an oxidative hit can produce. People who grey early have higher MDA, a breakdown product of oxidised linoleic acid, in their blood (Part II).15

Animal–in vitrofor lipid peroxides killing follicle cells; Inferredthat a diet high in seed oils speeds hair loss. There are no human studies of seed-oil intake and hair.

Pushback: the drugs are what works for pattern loss

The site's bias runs toward terrain over drugs, and the metabolic link in V.2 is real. But for pattern baldness, finasteride and minoxidil have multi-thousand-person placebo-controlled trials, and nothing in the natural column comes close. Pumpkin seed oil and rosemary each rest on one trial. The honest position is that fixing the terrain and the approved drugs aren't competing options. Someone losing hair to AGA who wants to keep it should know the drugs exist and what they cost in side effects, and decide.

PART VII

What To Do

VII.1For grey hair

  1. Test before supplementing. B12 (plus MMA if borderline), ferritin, TSH with free T3 and T4, 25-OH vitamin D, and copper with ceruloplasmin next to zinc. These are the places reversal has actually been documented (Part III). Stop biotin for a few days before the thyroid panel.
  2. Fix what's low, from food first. Copper: beef liver and oysters (see Copper, Freeze-Dried Oyster). B12: meat, liver, oysters, eggs (B12). Iron: red meat (Iron). Vitamin D: sun, then a supplement (Vitamin D, Sunlight). Nuts aren't needed for copper; liver and oysters are denser.
  3. If you're hypothyroid, get it treated. That's the best-documented reversal. Don't self-dose thyroid hormone to fix hair if your thyroid is normal.
  4. Don't smoke. It is one of the most consistent risk factors.16
  5. Manage stress, and expect a delay. Stress greying runs through the nerves; relief can let hairs near the threshold come back.7,8
  6. Lower the oxidative load the dull way: fewer seed oils (Linoleic Acid), enough protein, glycine and cysteine for glutathione (Glycine, Cysteine), and fruit.
  7. Optional, on mechanism only: NAC (~600–1,200 mg) is cheap and its antioxidant role is well established, but its grey-hair evidence is one knockout-mouse study. Pick it up for its other uses, not for hair.
  8. Set expectations by duration. Hairs that went grey recently, in someone young, after a clear stressor or deficiency, are the ones that come back. Hair white for decades usually stays white.

VII.2For hair loss

  1. Work out which kind. Pattern thinning over years is AGA. Sudden diffuse shedding two to three months after an event is TE.
  2. For shedding: find the trigger. Check ferritin, thyroid, vitamin D and zinc, and look at protein intake and recent crash dieting. It usually recovers within months once fixed.
  3. For pattern loss: minoxidil and finasteride are the evidence-based options. Microneedling, red light, pumpkin seed oil and rosemary oil are adjuncts with thinner evidence. Early crown balding is also worth a metabolic check (V.2).
  4. Cut seed oils and keep the oxidative load down. Oxidised linoleic acid kills follicle cells in mice, and oxidative stress carries DHT's signal (V.3–V.4).
  5. Protein and collagen: hair is keratin, cysteine-rich. Enough protein matters, and collagen is a reasonable glycine source. Its hair evidence is company-run (Glycine & Collagen).
Safety: more is not better
  • Zinc above ~40 mg/day long-term can cause copper deficiency. High-dose zinc depigmented mouse hair.33 Pair zinc with copper or keep it modest (Zinc).
  • Copper beyond sufficiency is pro-oxidant.11 Test, don't guess.
  • Vitamin A excess and selenium excess both cause hair loss.49 Hair-growth stacks often contain both (Vitamin A, Selenium).
  • Thyroid hormone in someone with a normal thyroid means palpitations, bone loss and arrhythmia risk. Self-dosing desiccated thyroid by armpit temperature, a protocol popular online, is a medical decision, not a hair tip.
  • Ashwagandha has case reports of liver injury and can raise thyroid hormone.
  • PABA in the reported studies was 12–24 g a day of a prescription drug.26 Levodopa is a Parkinson's drug.
  • Biotin throws off thyroid and some cardiac blood tests.

LIMITS

Limitations

  • The human greying data are small case-control studies. Dozens of people each, mostly South Asian and Middle Eastern populations where deficiencies are common. The associations may not transfer to a well-fed Western adult.
  • There is no independent, placebo-controlled trial of any supplement for grey hair. The one placebo-controlled trial found (Givaudan) was run by the ingredient's maker. Everything else is open-label, uncontrolled, or in mice.
  • Most mechanism work is in mice or cultured follicles. Mouse coat colour is a convenient readout but not a human scalp. The stress-nerve and NAC findings are mouse findings.
  • "Reversal" in case reports is selective. Case reports record the patients whose hair darkened, not the ones whose didn't.
  • The baldness part is a survey, not a full review. Finasteride and minoxidil side effects, PRP, dutasteride, ketoconazole, saw palmetto and female-specific treatments (spironolactone) are only touched on or left out.

What would move this paper most is a randomized trial in people with early, recent greying, testing a deficiency-correction protocol against placebo. That's the only intervention with a consistent human signal, and nobody has run it.

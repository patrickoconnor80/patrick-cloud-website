Dealer Gamma — Reading a GEX Heatmap

Options market makers hedge every contract they trade, and that hedging is real buying and selling in the underlying. Gamma decides whether their hedging calms a move down or makes it bigger; vanna and charm move their hedges even when price stands still. GEX heatmaps try to map where each happens, strike by strike.

Applies to SPY, QQQ, SPX, NDX, single stocksUpdated Nov 19, 2026
Not investment advice

An educational explainer. GEX figures from any vendor are model estimates, not observed dealer positions.

01

Delta: An Option Acts Like Shares

Every option has a delta: how much its price moves when the underlying moves $1. One contract covers 100 shares, so delta tells you how many shares the option behaves like.

Example

With SPY at $738, a 740 call might have a delta of 0.45. If SPY rises $1, the call gains about $0.45 per share, so one contract behaves like owning 45 shares. A 730 put might have a delta of −0.30, so it behaves like being short 30 shares.

Why dealers hedge

When a customer buys that call, a market maker (the “dealer”) sells it. The dealer earns the bid-ask spread and doesn't want a directional bet, so it buys 45 shares to offset the short call. That leaves it delta-neutral: price moves no longer affect its book in the first instance.

02

Gamma: How Fast Delta Changes

Delta doesn't stay fixed. As price moves toward a call's strike and past it, the call becomes more likely to pay off and its delta rises. Gamma is the rate of that change, and every change forces the dealer to trade more shares to stay neutral.

Where gamma is largest

At strikes near the current price, and on options close to expiration. A same-day (0DTE) option at the money can swing from a delta near 0.5 to near 0 or 1 within minutes. That's why the expiring-today and monthly-expiration columns of a heatmap carry most of the size, and later dates barely register.

03

Long Gamma vs. Short Gamma

Calls and puts both have positive gamma for whoever owns them. So the dealer's gamma doesn't depend on calls versus puts. It depends on whether the dealer bought or sold the option.

The same logic runs in reverse on the way down: a short-gamma dealer sells into falling prices, and a long-gamma dealer buys the dip.

The customer side is the mirror image

Every option has a buyer and a seller, so customer gamma is dealer gamma with the sign flipped. When retail piles into calls and institutions buy puts, customers are long gamma and dealers are short it, and the market gets jumpier. When covered-call funds and put writers dominate, customers are short gamma, dealers are long it, and the market calms down. The heatmap's colors are the dealer view: magenta means customers own the gamma, teal means customers sold it.

Who usually sits where

In index options (SPX, SPY, QQQ), the steady flows are institutions buying puts for protection and income funds selling calls, which is where the usual dealer short-puts, long-calls convention comes from. In single stocks, retail call buying dominates, so dealers are often short calls too. That's how heavy call buying in names like TSLA or NVDA can force dealers to buy stock into a rally (a “gamma squeeze”).

04

Calls or Puts: The Push Is the Same

If dealers are short options at a strike, it doesn't matter whether those options are calls or puts. Near that strike, their hedging pushes price further in whichever direction it is already moving. Take a strike of 730 with SPY trading just above it.

Dealers sold 730 calls

  • SPY drops toward 730. The calls are less likely to pay off, so the dealer needs fewer shares. It sells stock, pushing price lower.
  • SPY rises away from 730. The calls are more likely to pay off, so the dealer needs more shares. It buys stock, pushing price higher.

Sells falls, buys rises

Dealers sold 730 puts

  • SPY drops toward 730. The puts are more likely to pay off, so the dealer needs a bigger short. It sells stock, pushing price lower.
  • SPY rises away from 730. The puts are less likely to pay off, so the dealer needs a smaller short. It buys back stock, pushing price higher.

Sells falls, buys rises

Both cases produce the same flow. That is all “short gamma” means, and it is the only thing a negative (magenta) cell is telling you. Calls versus puts change only the starting hedge (a short call is hedged with long stock, a short put with short stock), and that matters later, when the options expire and the hedge is unwound (see section 06).

It amplifies; it doesn't start

Price can sit near a short-gamma strike and nothing happens. Something else (news, a large seller, a macro print) has to push it, and then dealer hedging adds fuel.

Strongest near the strike and near expiry

With SPY at $738, the 730 options matter only modestly. With SPY at $731 on expiration morning, they matter a great deal.

Only if dealers really are short

The heatmap assumes it. If dealers are actually long the 730 options, the effect reverses: they buy dips and sell rips, and 730 acts as a floor. Section 07 covers how to check.

05

The Other Greeks

Each Greek measures how an option's value or delta responds to one input. For dealer flows, what matters is anything that changes delta, because every change in delta is a hedge trade. Gamma does that through price; vanna through volatility; charm through time.

DeltaOption value per $1 move in the underlyingThe share-equivalent position the dealer hedges.
GammaChange in delta per $1 moveForces re-hedging as price moves. The sign decides whether hedging dampens or amplifies.
ThetaValue lost per day of time decayThe price of gamma. Option buyers pay theta for the right to profit from big moves; a short-gamma dealer collects it as compensation for chasing moves.
VegaValue change per 1-point change in implied volatilityDrives option prices around events (CPI, Fed, earnings). Doesn't itself force share trades.
RhoValue change per 1-point change in interest ratesMinor for short-dated options. Matters for LEAPS.

Vomma, speed, colorThird-order effects (vega's sensitivity to vol; gamma's sensitivity to price and time)Mostly for pricing desks. Color explains why a 0DTE strike's gamma spikes in the final hours.
Vanna in practice

Index puts are the most heavily bought options in the market, so the usual assumption is that dealers are short them and hold short stock (or short futures) as the hedge. When a feared event passes and implied volatility collapses, those puts lose delta, and dealers buy back their short hedge. That buying is the “vanna rally” often seen after a CPI print or Fed decision that turns out less bad than priced. It runs the other way too: a vol spike raises put deltas and forces more dealer selling, which is part of why selloffs feed on themselves.

VEX maps

Some tools chart vanna exposure (VEX) by strike, the same way GEX charts gamma. Gamma is concentrated in the nearest expirations and dominates the next zero to five days. Further out, gamma is spread thin and changes in implied volatility move dealer hedges more than price does, so swing trades lean on later columns and VEX, and day trades on the front columns and GEX.

Charm in practice

Each day, out-of-the-money options lose a bit of delta with no change in price or volatility. With dealers assumed short puts and long calls, both effects lead to dealer buying as expiration approaches: short-stock hedges on the puts get covered, and short-stock hedges against long calls shrink. Practitioners credit this steady bid for the tendency of index markets to drift higher into a monthly expiration.

06

Opex and the Hedge Unwind

Monthly options expiration (opex, the third Friday) concentrates months of open interest on one day. Two things happen to the hedges built around it: they decay into the date, then they disappear.

Illustrative. Price held constant; the hedge shrinks from time alone, then goes to zero when the contracts expire.

Into expiration: pin

Where dealers are long gamma at a big strike, their hedging pulls price toward it on expiration day. This is the effect with the strongest academic support (Ni, Pearson & Poteshman, 2005).

At expiration: unwind

Expired options need no hedge, so dealers close it. What they trade depends on what the hedge was. If the strike was put-heavy and dealers were short the puts, they held short stock and now buy it back. If it was call-heavy and dealers were short the calls, they held long stock and now sell it. This is where the call-versus-put split from section 04 finally matters.

After expiration: the window opens

Opex removes a large block of gamma at once. If that gamma was dampening moves, the market loses its stabilizer, and the week after a monthly opex often sees larger moves in either direction. Traders call it the “window of weakness,” because charm and vanna support that propped the market up into expiry stop with it.

Quarterly opex

March, June, September, and December expirations also roll index futures and settle large institutional hedges (the “quad witching” days). The unwind is larger and the aftermath is usually more volatile.

07

Long or Short? Figuring Out Dealer Positioning

Open interest says how many contracts exist at a strike, not who is long and who is short. No public source shows dealer books directly, so every estimate stacks several weaker clues.

  1. Fixed convention (most heatmaps) — assume customers buy puts for protection and sell calls against stock they own, so dealers are short puts and long calls. Call gamma is counted as positive, put gamma as negative. Simple, and roughly right for index options, but wrong at any strike where customers are doing the opposite.
  2. Trade direction (flow) — a fill at or above the ask usually means a customer bought, so the dealer sold. A fill at or below the bid usually means the dealer bought. Most “Flow” tabs are built this way. Spreads, multi-leg orders, and mid-market fills blur it.
  3. Opening versus closing — pair the flow with the next morning's change in open interest. Open interest up after ask-side buying means new customer longs, so dealers are newly short. Open interest down means positions were closed, and the hedge is coming off.
  4. Participant data — the OCC publishes volume by account type (customer, firm, market maker), and Cboe sells open/close data split by customer type. These are the closest thing to ground truth, but they are delayed, aggregated, or paid.
  5. Known structural players — covered-call ETFs (JEPI, JEPQ, QYLD and similar) sell index calls on a schedule, so dealers are long those calls. Pension and institutional hedgers buy index puts, so dealers are short those. Retail flow skews toward buying short-dated calls on single stocks, leaving dealers short them.
  6. Price behavior — the market's own reaction is a live check. If price keeps snapping back toward a strike, dealers are probably long gamma there. If it slices through and keeps going, they are probably short, whatever the map's color says.

For a single strike, check the raw call and put open interest for that expiration on any options chain. A strike dominated by puts under the usual convention reads as dealer short gamma with a short-stock hedge. A strike that is mostly calls, or roughly even, deserves skepticism about whatever sign the map assigns.

08

Reading the Heatmap

A GEX heatmap has strikes on the rows and expiration dates on the columns. Each cell estimates dealer gamma exposure at that strike, in dollars of hedging per 1% move.

Positive: dealers dampen movesNegative: dealers amplify movesFlip level

Stylized from the 0DTE column of a SPY heatmap on Nov 19, 2026 (SPY ~$738). Bar lengths are illustrative, not to scale.

Gamma flip

The strike where net gamma switches sign. Above it, dealer hedging tends to absorb moves and the session chops. Below it, hedging adds to the selling, so a break through the flip can turn a dip into a fast slide.

Large positive nodes

Read as magnets or walls. Dealers sell as price approaches from below and buy as it approaches from above, which tends to pin price nearby, especially on expiration day.

Large negative nodes

Read as acceleration zones. If price reaches one, hedging flows add to the move instead of absorbing it. Some traders also treat the biggest negative node as a target into expiration.

Size is relative

Read the largest node on the map first (often called the “King”); it sets the scale. A node matters only relative to it: $50M is huge on a small stock and invisible on SPX, and anything under roughly 20% of the largest node is usually background.

Above or below price

The same positive node does opposite jobs depending on where price is. Above price it caps rallies, because dealers sell as price climbs into it. Below price it cushions dips, because dealers buy as price falls into it.

Columns: add them up

Today's column (0DTE) has the most gamma per contract, but the monthly expiration usually has far more contracts, and the dollar figures in each cell already combine both. Dealers hedge their whole book, so what moves the market is the sum across columns at each strike. Columns weeks out matter little, because their gamma is spread thin.

Cells summed from the Nov 19, 2026 SPY heatmap (several values were truncated on screen, so the 11/20 totals are lower bounds). Even inside today's teal range, the opex column's short gamma outweighs it roughly four to one.

Worked example: Nov 19

Read alone, SPY's 0DTE column looked calm: positive from about 731 to 742, with the largest nodes at 740 (+$111M) and 736 (+$104M). But Friday's opex column was negative at every strike, with nodes of −$445M at 736, −$405M at 730, and −$548M at 725. Netted together, dealers were short gamma across the whole range, so the 0DTE “cushion” was much thinner than it looked. SPY closed at $715.87, down about 3%, straight through 730 and 725. Recomputed after the close from Yahoo option chains across all expirations within a week, the whole-book gamma flip sat near $739, just above where SPY opened, so the market spent the entire session in short gamma. The map explains why the selloff ran once it started. It couldn't say in advance that one would start.

Tomorrow the opex column gets stronger

Once the 11/19 contracts expire, 11/20 becomes the 0DTE column. Gamma on at-the-money options rises as expiration nears, so whatever short gamma sits near price on Friday morning hits hardest that day, then disappears at the close.

A drop through 730 is dealer selling, not short covering

Short covering means buying back a short, which pushes price up. A short-gamma slide is the opposite: dealers sell to keep up with their growing delta exposure as price falls. At 730, the map's −$405M implies roughly $405M of extra selling for every further 1% (about $7) drop near that strike, with more from 728, 726, and 725 below it. That is real fuel, but SPY alone trades tens of billions of dollars a day, and SPX options and S&P futures dwarf it, so it adds to a move rather than guaranteeing a crash. Short covering does show up later: after Friday's expiration, dealers who were short puts buy back their short-stock hedges (section 06).

09

What the Map Can't Know

GEX is a useful model with real limits. It's best used as context about how the market will react to a move, not as a forecast of direction.

Dealer positions are assumed

Every sign on the map rests on assumptions about who bought and who sold (section 07). If customers were heavily selling puts at a strike, dealers would actually be long gamma there, and the map would show the wrong color.

Stale inputs

Open interest updates once a day, overnight. Same-day 0DTE trading, now a large share of index options volume, mostly doesn't appear until the next morning.

Missing markets

A SPY-only map ignores SPX index options, which are far larger in notional. QQQ has the same problem with NDX. The Nasdaq-100 is also concentrated enough that gamma in single names like NVDA, AAPL, and MSFT can move QQQ more than QQQ's own options do.

What the evidence supports

Dealer gamma lines up better with volatility than with direction. Research finds that positive aggregate gamma tends to come with lower intraday volatility and negative gamma with larger, trending moves (Baltussen et al., 2021). Stocks with heavy open interest also tend to close near those strikes on expiration day (Ni, Pearson & Poteshman, 2005). Specific price targets are a much weaker claim, and the charts that get shared are the ones that worked.

10

Trading Around the Map

The defensible use of a GEX map is choosing the type of trade, not the direction. Long gamma favors selling premium around a pin. Short gamma favors owning movement or standing aside.

StructurePaid or collectedProfits whenFits
Long call or put butterfly (buy 1 low, sell 2 middle, buy 1 high)Pay a small debitPrice pins at the middle strike into expirationLong gamma, pin
Short iron butterfly (sell ATM call and put, buy wings)Collect a creditPrice stays near the middle strike; implied vol fallsLong gamma, pin; IV crush after events
Short call or put butterfly (the reverse of the long fly)Collect a small creditPrice moves away from the middle strikeShort gamma, breakout

The naming trips people up: a long butterfly and a short iron butterfly have the same payoff shape and both profit from a pin, while selling a plain butterfly is a bet on movement. To collect premium around a pin, the trade is the short iron fly, or the long butterfly bought for a debit.

Pin setup

On expiration day, ideally between late morning and mid-afternoon: net gamma across the expirations that matter is positive at spot, the largest positive node sits within about 0.5% of price, that node is a meaningful share of all nearby gamma, and its open interest is mostly calls (so the positive sign is credible under the usual convention). Center the butterfly on the node. A scheduled event that day can break a pin, so skip it on CPI and Fed days.

Event IV crush

Before CPI, a Fed decision, or a major earnings report, implied volatility rises, and the ATM straddle of the first expiration after the event prices the expected move. Selling an iron butterfly or iron condor before the event collects that inflated premium; it profits if the actual move is smaller than implied and vol collapses afterward. The risk is the event that is worse than priced, which lands in a short-gamma tape. Defined-risk wings are not optional here. Vanna adds a slight upward lean after a benign print, which is why some traders center the fly a little above spot.

Why wait for the break

Delta does change fastest near a strike, but dealers only trade after price moves: the hedge follows price, it doesn't lead it. With no push, a large short-gamma strike just sits there (apart from slow charm flows). And short gamma amplifies moves in both directions, so before price breaks, which way it accelerates is a coin flip. The flip level is also an estimate that moves with the method: one vendor's 0DTE view put SPY's flip near 731 on Nov 19, while the whole-book calculation put it near 739. Waiting for a close through the level confirms the push exists and which way it runs.

Keep score

Log every setup and outcome for a few weeks before sizing up. If a setup doesn't beat a coin flip after commissions over 30 or more trades, drop it.

11

Applying It to QQQ

  1. Find the flip in today's column — where it switches from positive to negative. Positive above means expect chop and dips getting bought. Negative below means expect momentum.
  2. Net it against the monthly opex column — during opex week, the third-Friday column often outweighs today's. Add them at each strike before deciding which regime you're in.
  3. Mark the largest node, then anything over ~20% of it — treat them as a likely range for the session, not as targets.
  4. Check the call/put split at the big nodes — it tells you whether the sign is believable and which way the unwind goes after expiration.
  5. Cross-check NDX and the megacaps — if the app shows NDX or NVDA gamma, the bigger book usually wins.
  6. Keep score — screenshot the map each morning, write down the call it implies, and compare it with the close. A few weeks of records beats a handful of memorable hits.

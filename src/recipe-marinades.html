10 Marinades

ChickenBeefPorkSeafoodGrillNo seed oils

Ten marinades made with olive oil, avocado oil or no oil at all. Marinating before you grill also cuts the carcinogens that form on charred meat.

From Cooking with Chris (@coookwithchris). Where the thread leaves out an amount of meat or a time, I filled it in and said so under that recipe. The nutrition numbers and the notes at the bottom are mine.

At a glance

Pick one, marinate tonight

Per 4 oz cooked serving of the default meat, plus its share of the marinade. ~ numbers are estimates: most of a thin marinade stays in the bag (see how it's counted). The marinades are sweet, so glaze them late.

How to marinate

  • 30 minutes for shrimp and fish, 2–8 hours for chicken and pork, 3–4 hours for skirt steak
  • Use glass or a zip bag in the fridge
  • Pat dry before grilling so it browns rather than steams
  • Glaze sweet marinades on near the end
why ↓

Watch out

  • Don't bake acidic marinades in foil
  • Most avocado oil sold in the US tested rancid or cut with other oils
  • Boil used marinade before you use it as a sauce

1Honey mustard chicken

Best on: Chicken · Marinate: 2–8 h · Batch: 2 lb chicken

  • ⅓ cupstone-ground mustard
  • ¼ cupraw honey
  • 2 tbspapple cider vinegar
  • 1 tbspextra virgin olive oil
  • 1 tspsalt
  • 1 tspblack pepper
  • 1 tsppaprika
  • 1 tspgarlic powder (or fresh garlic)
  • dashcayenne (optional)

Whisk everything together and coat about 2 lb of chicken. Marinate 2–8 hours, then grill or bake at 425 °F until done.

The thread gives no amount of meat, marinating time or cooking method. The 2 lb, the timing and the oven temperature are mine.

2Chipotle copycat

Best on: Chicken, steak · Marinate: 2–8 h · Batch: 2 lb chicken

  • ½ mediumred onion, diced
  • 4garlic cloves
  • 2 tbspolive oil
  • 3–4 tbspchipotles in adobo (or just the sauce)
  • 1 tbspchili powder
  • 1½ tspcumin
  • 1 tspdried oregano
  • 1 tspblack pepper
  • 2 tspsalt
  • ¼ cupwater

Blend until smooth, then marinate. Grill. It makes the chicken for burrito bowls.

The amount of meat and the marinating time are mine. USDA has no chipotles in adobo, so canned chili peppers stand in for them. The real thing is saltier, so the sodium here is an underestimate.

3Chimichurri shrimp

4Peruvian chicken

Best on: Whole chicken, skin-on · Marinate: 4–24 h · Batch: one 4 lb chicken

5Mediterranean salmon

Best on: Salmon · Marinate: Spread on, bake · Batch: 1½ lb salmon

  • zest of 1large lemon
  • juice of 2lemons
  • 3 tbspextra virgin olive oil
  • 5garlic cloves, chopped
  • 2 tspdried oregano
  • 1 tsppaprika
  • ½ tspblack pepper
  • to tastesalt, on the fish

Stir the marinade together. Pat the salmon dry and salt it well, then spread the marinade over it. Cover with unbleached parchment, not foil (see why), and bake at 375 °F for 10–20 minutes depending on thickness. Broil for the last 2–3 minutes if you want crisp edges, but don't burn the garlic. Pull at 125–130 °F inside and serve with parsley and lemon.

The 1½ lb of salmon and 1 tsp of salt are mine. 125–130 °F is a chef's medium. The FDA guideline is 145 °F, and using wild or previously frozen salmon covers the parasite concern at the lower temperature.

6Sticky garlic pork chops

Best on: Pork chops, chicken · Marinate: 1–4 h · Batch: 2 lb bone-in chops

  • ⅓ cuphoney, maple syrup or light brown sugar
  • 6–8garlic cloves, crushed
  • ¼ cuprice vinegar
  • 2 tbspfish sauce
  • 1 tbspsoy sauce or coconut aminos
  • 1 tbspketchup
  • ½ tspblack pepper
  • 2 tspsriracha

Marinate, then grill. Simmer the leftover marinade in a small pan until it thickens, and brush it on as a glaze near the end of cooking.

The amount of meat and the marinating time are mine. Rice vinegar is counted as plain vinegar. Boil the used marinade for a full minute before you glaze with it, because it has been sitting with raw meat.

8Mexican chicken

9Stir fry

Best on: Chicken, shrimp, beef · Marinate: 30 min–2 h · Batch: 1½ lb meat

  • ¼ cupcoconut aminos (or soy sauce)
  • 2 tspfish sauce
  • 2 clovesgarlic, grated
  • 1 tbspginger, grated
  • 2 tbspsriracha (to taste)
  • 2–4 tbsphoney (to taste)
  • pinchsalt

Use half the marinade on the meat. Stir-fry it, then pour in the other half at the very end and cook until it thickens.

The amounts of meat, garlic and ginger are mine. The numbers use soy sauce, so with coconut aminos the sodium will be lower.

10Carne asada

Best on: Skirt steak · Marinate: 3–4 h · Batch: 2 lb skirt steak

  • 1 bunchcilantro, finely chopped
  • 4garlic cloves, finely chopped
  • ½ cupavocado oil
  • 1 tbspcumin
  • 2 tspbrown sugar
  • 1 tsp eachsalt and pepper
  • zest & juice of 1lime
  • 2 tbspsoy sauce

Marinate 2 lb of skirt steak for 3–4 hours, then grill hot to the doneness you like. Slice against the grain.

Marinating cuts grilling carcinogens, but sugar complicates it

Grilling muscle meat at high heat produces heterocyclic amines (HCAs). These cause cancer in animals, and the long-term human effect is probably modest but not zero. The most abundant one in grilled chicken is PhIP. Lawrence Livermore researchers grilled chicken breasts with and without a marinade very close to Chris's honey mustard: brown sugar, olive oil, cider vinegar, garlic, mustard, lemon juice and salt.[1] Marinating cut PhIP by 92–99%. Total HCAs after 30 minutes on the grill fell from 158 to 10 ng/g.

The catch is sugar. Another HCA, MeIQx, rose more than tenfold in the marinated chicken at 30–40 minutes of grilling. When they tested one ingredient at a time, sugar caused it. At 40 minutes the marinated chicken was more mutagenic than the plain chicken. A Kansas State study of beef steaks found the same benefit without that downside: three spice-and-herb marinades with no sugar cut total HCAs by 57–88%, and even the plain oil-vinegar base helped.[2]

In practice: marinate everything. For the four sweet marinades (honey mustard, sticky garlic, maple dijon, stir fry), cook over moderate heat and add the glaze in the last few minutes. The sticky garlic recipe already does this. Don't take any of them to char. (Moderate: consistent lab measurements across two groups, but no human outcome data)

Foil and lemon: he's right

A University of Milan group baked beef, chicken and fish three ways: in a Pyrex dish, wrapped in foil, and wrapped in foil with oil, salt and lemon.[3] Foil raised aluminum in every food. The highest levels, about 40 mg/kg, came from the seasoned fish and chicken. Electron microscopy showed larger and more numerous holes eaten into the foil where it touched seasoned food. Acids and salt bind aluminum and pull it out of the foil.

For scale: a 6 oz piece of fish at 42 mg/kg carries about 7 mg. That's around a tenth of the weekly tolerable intake for a 155 lb adult (EFSA: 1 mg per kg of body weight per week). An earlier Turkish study of meats baked in foil, without acid, found aluminum rose 76–378% but judged there was "no evident risk" from that alone.[4] One foil-baked salmon isn't a problem. Doing it every week adds to aluminum you already get from other sources, and parchment avoids the issue at no cost. (Moderate: direct measurements, small samples)

No seed oils, but check the avocado oil and the chicken

Every recipe here uses olive oil or avocado oil, and four use none. The marinades add little linoleic acid. The serving's share of oil is a teaspoon or two, and both oils are only about 10–12% linoleic acid. The meat matters far more. In the panel above, a 4 oz serving of the Peruvian chicken (skin-on) carries 3.7 g of linoleic acid, about 9.5% of its calories, because conventional chicken fat comes from corn and soy feed. Chicken thigh is about 7%. Breast is 3%. Skirt steak, flank, salmon and shrimp are 1–2%. On the Linoleic Acid paper's terms, the beef, salmon and shrimp recipes are the low-omega-6 picks. For the chicken recipes, use breast, or pastured birds if you can find them.

The weak point is avocado oil itself. UC Davis tested 22 retail avocado oils.[5] Fifteen had oxidized before their expiration date and six had been mixed with other oils. Three bottles labeled "pure" or "extra virgin" were nearly 100% soybean oil, which is exactly what this list avoids.

For the Mexican chicken and the carne asada, which each use ½ cup, buy a brand that publishes third-party purity tests. Or use extra virgin olive oil, which works in both recipes. (Moderate: one retail survey, 22 samples)
